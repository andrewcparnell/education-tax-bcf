# Peer Review Report: *Does Increasing Government Spending on Tertiary Education Provide a Return on Investment?* (Post-ROI Reframing Adversarial Audit)

## Summary and Overall Verdict

This manuscript asks whether increasing public spending on tertiary education provides a positive fiscal return on investment (ROI) over 5-year and 10-year horizons across an unbalanced panel of 121 countries (`1990-2024`), using a custom C++ (`RcppArmadillo`) continuous-treatment Multivariate Bayesian Causal Forest (MV-BCF) model across four disaggregated tax outcomes ($Y_1$ to $Y_4$) and four university funding definitions ($Z_1$ to $Z_4$). In its latest revision, the paper reframes its central question around fiscal Return on Investment (ROI), introduces a cohort-accounting translation that compares a single entering university cohort's multi-year degree cost against its cumulative 40-year career tax output (`8x` the Year-10 annual flow), and adds a policy-maker lookup guide and scatter plot in Section 5.4 and Appendix F. The paper has many admirable strengths: its decomposition of the UNESCO denominator trap ($Z_1$ vs $Z_2, Z_3, Z_4$) is genuinely insightful, the C++ MCMC implementation and out-of-sample country-blocked validation are transparent and reproducible, and the distinction between short-run student lock-in at 5 years and graduate labour-market maturation at 10 years is well motivated.

**Overall Verdict: Major Revision.** Focusing strictly on major methodological, econometric, and fiscal-accounting problems that a sceptical referee or treasury economist could use to argue that key claims of the paper are invalid, I identify **seven potentially fatal or near-fatal vulnerabilities** below. None of these concerns are minor stylistic quibbles; each targets a core pillar of the causal identification or the new 40-year Return on Investment headline claims.

---

## Major Concerns (Potential Invalidating Flaws)

1. **Mechanical Independence of $\tau_{10}(W_i)$ from Enrolment Share Creates Implausible 40-Year Benefit-Cost Ratios ($10\times$ to $74\times$) in Low-Enrolment Developing Countries (Section 5.4 and Appendix F).**
   - *The core flaw:* Look closely at the algebra connecting the treatment variable $Z_1$ (per-student university spending as a percentage of GDP per person) to the Cohort Input Cost and the 40-year Career Tax Output. By definition, $\text{Spend}_{i} = \frac{\text{Budget}_i / \text{Students}_i}{\text{GDP}_i / \text{Pop}_i}$. When a country increases $\text{Spend}_i$ by $+10$ percentage points of GDP per person per student, the public cost as a share of GDP is:
     $$\text{Cohort Input Cost (\% of GDP)} = \text{Budget\_GDP}_i \times \frac{10}{\text{Spend}_i} = 10 \times \text{Cohort Factor} \times \text{Gross Enrolment}_i$$
     Notice that the Cohort Input Cost (% of GDP) is **strictly proportional to the country's university enrolment rate** $\text{Enrolment}_i$: in a country where only $3\%$ to $5\%$ of young adults attend university (such as Rwanda, Burkina Faso, Togo, or Bangladesh), raising spending by $+10$ pp of GDP per person on each of those few students costs only $0.03\%$ to $0.19\%$ of GDP. However, in the MV-BCF model, the predicted national tax return $\tau_{10}(W_i)$ (% of national GDP) does *not* scale down proportionally with $\text{Enrolment}_i$: in fact, Table 3 shows that $\tau_{10}$ on $Y_1$ is *larger* in low-enrolment ($<20\%$) countries ($+0.34$ pp of national GDP) than in high-enrolment ($\ge 50\%$) countries ($+0.17$ pp of national GDP)!
   - *Why this can invalidate the developing-country ROI claims:* Dividing a $+0.34\%$ of national GDP annual tax gain ($2.7\%$ of GDP over 40 years) by a $0.03\%$ to $0.07\%$ of GDP input cost mechanically produces a 40-year Benefit-Cost Ratio of $41.4\times$ in Rwanda and $74.3\times$ in Burkina Faso! A public-finance referee will immediately object that educating $2\%$ of the population at slightly higher quality cannot possibly raise *aggregate national tax revenue* by $+0.34\%$ of total GDP per year unless the per-graduate tax return exceeds each graduate's entire lifetime income. Either $Z_1$ in low-enrolment countries is proxying for broader state-capacity investments, or $\tau(W_i)$ for per-student spending $Z_1$ must be multiplied by enrolment share when translating per-student treatment intensity into aggregate GDP points.

2. **Headline Treasury ROI Relies on Total Non-Resource Tax ($Y_1$), Where Within-OECD Slopes Are Negative and Out-of-Sample Predictive Gain Over the Historical Mean Is Zero (Sections 5.4, 5.5, and Appendix C).**
   - *The core flaw:* Section 5.4 advises national treasuries (using Ireland and the United Kingdom as flagship examples) to evaluate university ROI primarily using Total Non-Resource Tax ($Y_1$, where Ireland's 40-year BCR is $4.08\times$ and $P_{\text{net}} = 0.77$) rather than Direct Income Tax ($Y_2$, where Ireland's 40-year BCR is $1.21\times$ and $P_{\text{net}} = 0.54$). Yet three pieces of evidence elsewhere in the paper undermine $Y_1$ as a reliable causal return in high-income OECD economies:
     1. In **Appendix Table 3 (Rows 8 to 12)**, when the MV-BCF model is estimated on the 119 OECD country-waves alone (`N = 119`, 37 OECD countries), the $+10$-year posterior mean slope on $Y_1$ is **negative** across all five OECD tuition regimes ($-0.14$ to $-0.17$ pp of GDP), whereas $Y_2$ remains positive ($+0.04$ to $+0.09$ pp) and $Y_3$ is strongly positive ($+1.05$ to $+1.14$ pp, $P = 0.93\text{--}0.95$). Thus, Ireland's positive $+0.22$ pp slope on $Y_1$ (`€9.66B` career output) comes entirely from pooling the 119 OECD waves with 164 non-OECD developing waves and shrinking the OECD leaf toward the global mean!
     2. In **Section 5.5 (Figure 7)**, 5-fold country-blocked cross-validation shows that on $Y_1$, the unconditional historical training mean ($\text{CRPS} = 1.60$ pp, $\text{RMSE} = 3.05$ pp) outperforms MV-BCF ($\text{CRPS} = 1.64$ pp, $\text{RMSE} = 3.15$ pp), whereas MV-BCF substantially beats the mean on $Y_3$ ($3.41$ vs $3.63$ pp).
     3. In **Section 5.1 (Table 1)**, the global direct slope on $Y_1$ has a 95% credible interval of $[-0.15, +0.70]$ pp ($P = 0.89$), and for Ireland the 40-year career 95% CI on $Y_1$ spans `[€-10.3B, €+31.9B]`.
   - *Why this matters:* A referee will argue that telling an OECD Finance Minister they will get a $4\times$ to $5\times$ return on $Y_1$ is fragile when the OECD-only sub-model estimates a negative $Y_1$ slope and out-of-sample CV shows no predictive skill on $Y_1$ beyond the unconditional mean.

3. **Confusing a Persistent Funding Level ($Z_1$) with a Single 5-Year Cohort Impulse in the 40-Year Career Multiplier (`8x Year-10 Flow`, Sections 3.3, 5.1, and 5.4).**
   - *The core flaw:* The main specification (`M2b`) regresses the 10-year *change* in tax outcomes ($\Delta Y_{i,t\to t+2} = L_{i,t+2} - L_{i,t}$) on the *level* of per-student university spending in wave $t$ ($Z_{1,i,t}$), not the 5-year change in spending ($Z_{2,i,t} = \Delta Z_{i,t-1\to t}$). In panel data, per-student spending levels $Z_{1,i,t}$ are highly persistent within countries: a country with $Z_{1,i,t} = +1.0$ in wave $t$ (`2000-2004`) typically also had $Z_{1} \approx +1.0$ in wave $t+1$ (`2005-2009`).
   - *Why this can invalidate the cohort cost accounting:* When $\Delta Y_{i,t\to t+2}$ is regressed on $Z_{1,i,t}$ without controlling for future spending $Z_{1,i,t+1}$, the coefficient $\tau_{10}$ captures the cumulative 10-year fiscal effect of **two consecutive five-year waves of higher spending** (10 cohorts educated during wave $t$ *and* wave $t+1$), not a one-off 5-year impulse in wave $t$ that reverts to zero in wave $t+1$! If $\tau_{10}$ reflects two five-year waves of higher university spending (10 cohorts in the labour force by the end of wave $t+2$), then each single cohort's annual contribution at Year 10 is $\tau_{10} / 10$ rather than $\tau_{10} / 5$, which would **halve** the 40-year career multiplier from $8\times$ to $4\times$ (and halve all BCRs in Section 5.4 and Appendix F)! Conversely, if one wants the impulse response to a 5-year funding shock, one should use $Z_2$ (`M3b_Delta10pp_10y`) or control for $Z_{i,t+1}$ via sequential g-estimation.

4. **Zero Discounting and Absence of General-Equilibrium Skill-Premium Compression in the 40-Year Extrapolation (Section 5.4).**
   - *The core flaw:* Even if $\tau_{10} / 5$ is accepted as the single-cohort annual tax gain at Year 10, multiplying it by $40$ working years ($8\times$ the five-cohort Year-10 flow) makes two heroic economic assumptions:
     1. **Zero real discounting ($\rho = 0$) relative to GDP growth:** Government treasuries evaluate public investments by discounting future cash flows at the real sovereign borrowing cost or social time preference rate $r$ (for example, $3.0\%$ to $4.0\%$ real in the UK Green Book and Irish Public Spending Code). Even if tax revenues grow in line with real GDP growth $g \approx 1.5\text{--}2.0\%$, as long as $r > g$ (dynamic efficiency), discounting across years $5$ to $44$ after university entry reduces the present-value multiplier from $8.0\times$ to roughly $5.2\times\text{--}6.0\times$.
     2. **Partial-equilibrium extrapolation without diminishing returns across cohorts:** While one better-funded cohort may capture higher formal salaries and pay more income tax, graduating 40 consecutive cohorts at higher funding levels massively expands the aggregate supply of university-educated workers, which in macro general equilibrium compresses the college wage premium and displaces non-graduates from formal salaried roles.
   - *Note:* The authors have added a welcome caveat in Section 5.4 acknowledging that the 40-year extrapolation is speculative, but a treasury referee will still ask to see how the BCR changes under a standard $r - g = 1.5\%$ or $2.0\%$ net discount rate (where $8.0\times$ becomes $\approx 5.7\times$).

5. **Single-Stage Conditioning on Contemporaneous Post-Treatment Mediators ($M_{i,t\to t+h}$) in the Primary Specification `M2b` Invites Collider Bias (Section 4.3).**
   - *The core flaw:* The primary specification `M2b` places four follow-up variables realised over $[t, t+2]$ - real GDP per person growth ($M_1$), tertiary enrolment growth ($M_2$), Gini change ($M_3$), and life expectancy change ($M_4$) - directly inside the prognostic forest $\mu(X_{i,t}, M_{i,t\to t+2}, \hat{z}_{i,t})$. As shown by Acharya, Blackwell, and Sen (2016) and Imai et al. (2010), conditioning directly on post-treatment variables $M_{i,t\to t+2}$ in a single-stage regression induces **collider stratification bias** whenever any unobserved shock $U_{i,t\to t+2}$ over the 10-year window (such as a commodity boom, trade liberalisation, or institutional reform) jointly affects both real GDP growth $M_1$ and tax revenues $Y_{i,t+2}$.
   - *Mitigation already in the paper, and what remains vulnerable:* To the author's credit, the paper reports the Total Effect without mediators (`M1b`, which gives $+0.30$ pp on $Y_1$, $+0.16$ pp on $Y_2$, and $+0.54$ pp on $Y_3$) and two-stage sequential g-estimation (`R9`, which demediates $Y$ first and gives $+0.46$ pp on $Y_1$, $+0.20$ pp on $Y_2$, and $+0.85$ pp on $Y_3$). Nevertheless, keeping the naive single-stage post-treatment-conditioned model `M2b` as the *primary* specification throughout the paper is a target for any causal inference referee, who would argue that `M1b` (Total Effect) or `R9` (Sequential G-Estimation) is theoretically cleaner.

6. **Unclustered i.i.d. Likelihood in the C++ MV-BCF Sampler With Overlapping 10-Year Panel Waves (Section 4.3 and Appendix A).**
   - *The core flaw:* In the $+10$-year panel ($N = 283$ across 112 countries), each country contributes up to five rolling 5-year waves (`1990-1994 -> 2000-2004`, `1995-1999 -> 2005-2009`, etc.). Consecutive 10-year first differences $\Delta Y_{i,t\to t+2}$ and $\Delta Y_{i,t+1\to t+3}$ share an overlapping 5-year window (`[t+1, t+2]`), which mechanically induces a moving-average $\text{MA}(1)$ serial correlation structure in the error terms $\varepsilon_{i,t+2}$ within each country.
   - *Why the current fix is incomplete:* The C++ sampler in Equation (4) updates the trees and the $4 \times 4$ covariance matrix $\Sigma$ under the assumption $\varepsilon_{i,t+2} \stackrel{\text{i.i.d.}}{\sim} \mathcal{N}_4(0, \Sigma)$ across all 283 rows, treating repeated overlapping waves from the same country as independent observations when computing leaf precisions $\Omega_{\tau,b}^{-1}$ and tree acceptance ratios. While Check `R2` (`N = 154` non-overlapping decadal waves) and `clu_prob_pos` in Table A1 provide reassurance that the point estimates remain positive, `clu_prob_pos` only adjusts for between-country variance across the country-level posterior means rather than embedding a country-clustered or $\text{MA}(1)$ error covariance inside the Bayesian likelihood itself.

7. **Stale 2015–2019 UNESCO Per-Student Spending Ratios Paired With 2020–2024 Fiscal and GDP Figures in the Policy Maker Lookup Table (Appendix F).**
   - *The core flaw:* As acknowledged in Appendix F, the World Bank archive of UNESCO's disaggregated per-student tertiary spending series (`SE.XPD.TERT.PC.ZS`) ends in `2018-2019`. Consequently, Table 10 in Appendix F (and the web app) pairs each country's `2015-2019` per-student spending ratio with its `2020-2024` GDP, population, enrolment, and tax revenue. For countries that significantly expanded or contracted university funding between 2018 and 2024 (for instance, due to post-COVID inflation eroding real per-student spending or major tertiary reforms), the baseline spending ratio and the resulting Cohort Input Cost (`Budget_GDP * 10 / Spend_raw`) could be noticeably out of date.
