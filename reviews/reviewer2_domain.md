# Referee Report: Reviewer 2 (Public Economics, Fiscal Capacity & Higher Education Finance)

**Manuscript Title:** *Does Increasing Government Spend on Tertiary Education Improve the Future Tax Take? A Bayesian Causal Inference Analysis*  
**Files Reviewed:** `paper/paper.tex`, `paper/paper.md`, `paper/references.bib`, `literature/literature.csv`, `data/processed/data_dictionary.csv`, and supporting pipeline scripts (`src/r/01_download_tax_education_data.R` to `src/r/06_generate_figures_and_diagnostics.R`).

### 1. Overall impression

This manuscript tackles an important and policy-relevant question at the intersection of public economics and higher education finance: whether national government expenditure on tertiary education generates a genuine long-run fiscal dividend by expanding a country's direct income and corporate profit tax base beyond general macroeconomic growth. The conceptual framing is intelligent and well motivated. Linking the microeconomic literature on "institutional crowding" and per-student instructional resources (Bound et al., 2010; Deming and Walters, 2017; Chakrabarti et al., 2020) to the public-finance literature on third-party wage reporting, firm formalisation, and modern tax structure (Kleven et al., 2016; Pomeranz and Vila-Belda, 2019; Jensen, 2022) provides a plausible structural mechanism for why university funding should specifically shift revenue toward direct income and profit taxes while leaving indirect consumption tax shares flat or declining. Avoiding mechanical GDP product identities by modelling four disaggregated fiscal ratios jointly via Multivariate and Three-Wave Longitudinal Bayesian Causal Forests (`mvbcf` and `lbcf`), and unbundling the standard UNESCO per-student ratio into real purchasing-power-parity (PPP) dollars per student and aggregate tertiary budget shares of GDP, are genuine methodological strengths. The codebase is openly provided, compiles cleanly in C++ (`RcppArmadillo`), and contains zero hallucinated references across the 46-item bibliography.

Despite these strengths, a close audit of the data-construction pipeline, institutional accounting perimeters, cohort maturation timing, and subgroup results reveals five major substantive and methodological flaws that prevent publication in its current form. Most seriously, although the manuscript, data dictionary, and Data Availability Statement repeatedly claim that domestic non-resource tax revenue is sourced from or harmonised with the UNU-WIDER Government Revenue Dataset (GRD), inspection of `data/raw/PROVENANCE.md` and `src/r/02_build_clean_tax_panel.R` shows that no UNU-WIDER GRD data are ever downloaded or merged; instead, the code applies an undocumented, ad hoc arithmetic subtraction of World Bank natural resource rents to IMF/WDI budgetary central government series, creating a severe central-versus-general government perimeter mismatch against UNESCO general-government education spending and mismeasuring direct income tax to GDP. Furthermore, the five-year and ten-year horizons do not cleanly match realistic university cohort completion and peak tax-bracket entry timelines, the intensive-versus-extensive margin horserace in Table 3 compares models across non-identical country samples ($N = 283$, $N = 176$, and $N = 403$) using 40-year pooled income-group medians, critical developing-country fiscal leakage channels (graduate brain drain and tax enforcement capacity) and low-income subgroup rows are omitted, and the causal mediation and "self-financing within a decade" claims overreach the evidence (particularly given that the $+10$-year total effect `M1` and the $+10$-year within-country fixed-effects check `R3b` both have 95% credible intervals that comfortably span zero). I therefore recommend **major revision** (or rejection with invitation to resubmit once the data provenance and institutional perimeter issues are rectified).

### 2. Major concerns

1. **Misstated UNU-WIDER GRD provenance, ad hoc resource-rent subtraction, and central-versus-general government perimeter mismatch.**  
   Throughout the manuscript (`paper.tex` Section 3.1 line 121, Section 3.2 line 130, and Data Availability Statement line 571) and in `data/processed/data_dictionary.csv` (lines 19 and 20), the author states that overall non-resource tax revenue is constructed from the UNU-WIDER Government Revenue Dataset (Prichard, 2016; ICTD/UNU-WIDER, 2023). However, an inspection of `data/raw/PROVENANCE.md`, `src/r/01_download_tax_education_data.R`, and `src/r/02_build_clean_tax_panel.R` (lines 199-208) shows that **no UNU-WIDER GRD dataset is present anywhere in the repository**. Instead, `non_resource_tax_gdp_pct` is fabricated from World Bank WDI indicators (`GC.TAX.TOTL.GD.ZS` and `GC.REV.XGRT.GD.ZS`) using an undocumented piecewise formula:
   $$\text{NonResTax/GDP} = \max\bigl(\text{GC.TAX.TOTL.GD.ZS} - 0.5 \max(\text{ResourceRents/GDP} - 10, 0),\, 2.0\bigr),$$
   with a fallback to $\max(\text{GC.REV.XGRT.GD.ZS} - 0.7 \times \text{ResourceRents/GDP},\, 2.0)$ when `GC.TAX.TOTL.GD.ZS` is missing. From a public economics perspective, this creates three critical errors:
   - *First (Provenance accuracy):* Attributing an ad hoc WDI subtraction rule to the UNU-WIDER GRD is factually incorrect. Moreover, falling back to `GC.REV.XGRT.GD.ZS` (total government revenue excluding grants) conflates non-tax revenues (such as state-owned enterprise dividends, administrative fees, and property income) with tax revenues.
   - *Second (Institutional perimeter mismatch):* WDI's `GC.TAX.*` indicators cover **budgetary central government** only, excluding state, provincial, regional, and municipal taxation. By contrast, UNESCO UIS education indicators (`SE.XPD.*`) consolidate **general government** expenditure across central, state/provincial, and local governments (and true UNU-WIDER GRD data specifically harmonise general government revenue). In federal and decentralised countries where higher education funding and personal or corporate income taxation are heavily sub-national (for example, the United States, Canada, Germany, Switzerland, Brazil, and India), pairing general-government university spending with central-government tax receipts distorts both levels and changes.
   - *Third (Denominator mismatch and resource profit taxes in Outcome 2):* In `02_build_clean_tax_panel.R` (lines 205-206), Outcome 2 (`inc_tax_gdp_pct`) is calculated by multiplying `non_resource_tax_gdp_pct` (% of GDP in taxes) by `GC.TAX.YPKG.ZS`, which is taxes on income, profits, and capital gains as a percentage of **total central government revenue** (including non-tax revenue and grants), rather than `GC.TAX.YPKG.RV.ZS` (% of total taxes). Multiplying a share of total revenue by tax-to-GDP understates direct income tax as a share of GDP whenever non-tax revenues or social contributions exist. Worse, in resource-rich countries, oil and mining taxes are largely collected as corporate profit taxes inside `GC.TAX.YPKG.ZS`; unlike true UNU-WIDER GRD series (which isolate non-resource direct taxes), this formula leaves volatile resource corporate taxes inside Outcome 3 (`Direct Income Tax Share`) and Outcome 2 (`Direct Income Tax / GDP`).
   
   *Actionable fix:* The author must either (a) genuinely download and merge the UNU-WIDER GRD v2025 general-government non-resource tax and non-resource direct income/profit tax series into `data/raw/` and re-run the pipeline, or (b) remove all claims that UNU-WIDER GRD data are used in the empirical dataset, write out the exact WDI resource-rent adjustment equations in Section 3.2, replace `GC.TAX.YPKG.ZS` with `GC.TAX.YPKG.RV.ZS` (or direct IMF GFS general-government income-tax-to-GDP series) when computing `inc_tax_gdp_pct`, and add an explicit discussion of the central-versus-general government perimeter mismatch (along with a federal-country sensitivity check).

2. **Mismatch between $+5\text{y}$/$+10\text{y}$ wave horizons and realistic university cohort maturation timelines, and unexplained divergence between `mvbcf` and `lbcf` on `Direct Income Tax / GDP`.**  
   Because annual observations are averaged into five-year waves (`1985-1989` through `2020-2024`), a funding expansion in wave $t$ (say `2000-2004`, midpoint 2002) is evaluated in wave $t+1$ (`2005-2009`, midpoint 2007) and wave $t+2$ (`2010-2014`, midpoint 2012). Consider a student cohort entering a three-to-five-year degree programme in the middle or latter half of wave $t$ (2002-2004): those students only graduate between 2005 and 2009 (during wave $t+1$) and are only 23 to 29 years old during wave $t+2$. Labour economics research shows that the college wage premium and progression into upper personal income tax brackets (or formal managerial roles) steepen in workers' thirties and forties, after postgraduate study and early-career job mobility. Consequently, the large $+1.17$ percentage point jump in `Direct Income Tax Share` ($P = 0.982$) and $+0.20$ pp rise in `Direct Income Tax / GDP` ($P = 0.941$) already observed at the **$+5$-year horizon (`t+1`)** cannot plausibly be explained solely by newly minted graduates entering peak tax brackets. At $+5$ years, much of that effect likely reflects an immediate Keynesian and public-payroll channel: higher government university spending directly pays formal salaries to faculty, researchers, and university staff (whose earnings are subject to immediate PAYE income-tax withholding) and procures goods and services from formal domestic contractors.
   
   Furthermore, there is an unaddressed contradiction between the cross-sectional `mvbcf` results (Table 2) and the longitudinal `lbcf` results (Table 5) on **Outcome 2 (`Direct Income Tax / GDP`)**. Whereas `mvbcf` (`M2`) estimates positive effects of $+0.20$ pp at $+5$ years ($P = 0.94$) and $+0.28$ pp at $+10$ years ($P = 0.92$), the three-wave longitudinal `lbcf` model (Table 5, Row 2) estimates a **negative** initial $+5$-year effect ($\tau_1 = -0.06$ pp, $P = 0.36$) and an imprecise cumulative $+10$-year effect of only $+0.14$ pp (95% CrI $[-0.20, +0.50]$, $P = 0.79$). If `lbcf` finds a cumulative $+0.73$ pp increase in overall `Non-Resource Tax / GDP` ($P = 0.98$) but only $+0.14$ pp from `Direct Income Tax / GDP` ($P = 0.79$) and $+0.06$ pp from `Goods & Services Share`, what tax instrument accounts for the other $+0.59$ pp of GDP in `lbcf`? In addition, inspection of `src/r/05_run_longitudinal_bcf_tax.R` (lines 94-98) reveals that `lbcf` only includes two of the four mediators (`d_t1_log_gdp_pc_pct` and `d_t1_tert_enrol_pp`), omitting the forward Gini inequality and life-expectancy mediators included in `mvbcf` Model `M2`.
   
   *Actionable fix:* (a) Harmonise the mediator vector in `05_run_longitudinal_bcf_tax.R` with `04_run_mvbcf_tax_models.R` so `lbcf` conditions on all four mediators; (b) explicitly discuss in Section 5.3 why `Direct Income Tax / GDP` is much weaker in `lbcf` ($+0.14$ pp, $P = 0.79$) than in `mvbcf` ($+0.28$ pp, $P = 0.92$) and decompose which other revenue categories drive the $+0.73$ pp `Non-Resource Tax / GDP` gain in `lbcf`; and (c) add a $+15$-year ($t+3$) horizon estimate (even on the smaller available sample) while explicitly distinguishing immediate $+5$-year university payroll/withholding effects from $+10$-to-$+15$-year graduate labour-market maturation.

3. **Confounded intensive-versus-extensive margin horserace (Table 3), look-ahead bias in 40-year pooled income-group medians, and internal inconsistency in `M3d` (Real PPP \$ per student).**  
   Section 5.2 (Table 3 and Figure 3) argues that intensive-margin per-student funding (`M2b` UNESCO ratio, `M3b` observed per-student expansion, `M3d` real PPP dollars per student) drives the fiscal dividend, whereas extensive-margin aggregate tertiary budget share (`M3f`, `% of GDP`) yields weak returns ($+0.21$ pp on Non-Resource Tax / GDP, $+0.10$ pp on Direct Income Tax / GDP). While theoretically appealing, the empirical comparison in Table 3 suffers from three methodological flaws:
   - *First (Non-comparable sample perimeters):* In Table 3, `M2b` and `M3d` are fitted on $N = 283$ waves (112 countries), `M3b` is fitted on $N = 176$ waves (86 countries), and `M3f` (`tert_spend_gdp_pct`) is fitted on $N = 403$ waves (123 countries). Because `M3f` includes 120 additional country-waves where UNESCO headcount breakdown is missing (disproportionately earlier waves and developing countries), the weaker point estimate in Row 4 ($+0.21$ pp vs $+0.53$ pp) conflates the change in treatment definition with a 42% change in sample composition.
   - *Second (Time-invariant 40-year pooled income-group medians):* In `src/r/02_build_clean_tax_panel.R` (lines 228-236), the binary regime treatments (`Z_regime_inc`, `Z_real_spend_regime`, `Z_tert_gdp_regime`) are defined by comparing each country-wave against the median of its World Bank income group **pooled across all eight waves from 1985 to 2024**, using a single static 2024 income-group classification (`wb_countries_metadata.csv`). Because real PPP spending per student and tertiary budget shares rose substantially over 40 years (and countries such as China, South Korea, Chile, and Poland transitioned across income groups between 1985 and 2024), computing medians across all 40 years and conditioning on end-of-sample (2024) income group status introduces look-ahead information into $Z_{i,t}$ and classifies early waves (1985-1994) as "low real PPP spending" simply due to four decades of secular economic growth.
   - *Third (Unexplained divergence in `M3d` between Tax/GDP and Income Tax Share):* In Table 3, Row 3 (`M3d`, Real 2021 PPP dollars per student), the $+10$-year effect on `Non-Resource Tax / GDP` doubles to $+1.06$ pp ($P = 0.99$), yet the effect on `Direct Income Tax Share` drops by more than half from $+1.66$ pp ($P = 0.98$) in `M2b` to $+0.74$ pp ($P = 0.79$, 95% CrI $[-1.02, +2.55]$). The text highlights the $+1.06$ pp Tax/GDP result while glossing over why real PPP per-student spending fails to produce a statistically credible shift in the direct income tax share ($P = 0.79$), or what non-income tax revenue accounts for the remaining $+0.70$ pp of the $+1.06$ pp Tax/GDP increase.
   
   *Actionable fix:* (a) Re-estimate all four exposures in Table 3 on the exact common intersection sample ($N = 283$, or $N = 176$ when comparing against expansions) alongside the full samples, and fit a specification that includes intensive-margin per-student spending and extensive-margin budget share simultaneously; (b) compute income-group thresholds strictly within each $(\text{income\_group}, \text{wave\_index})$ cell (or use pre-treatment wave-$t$ GDP per person quartiles) so $Z_{i,t}$ contains zero future information; (c) report the results for `Z_real_spend_expansion` and `Z_tert_gdp_expansion` (which are already constructed in `02_build_clean_tax_panel.R` lines 269-279 but omitted from the paper); and (d) explain why `M3d` yields $+0.74$ pp ($P = 0.79$) on `Direct Income Tax Share`.

4. **Omission of graduate emigration ("brain drain"), tax enforcement capacity, and low-income subgroup rows from the heterogeneity analysis.**  
   In developing economies, two well-established fiscal mechanisms determine whether public higher education spending translates into domestic income tax revenue:
   - *Skilled emigration (brain drain):* In low-income, lower-middle-income, and small-state economies, between 20% and 60% of university graduates emigrate to OECD labour markets (Docquier and Rapoport, 2012; Gibson and McKenzie, 2011). When publicly subsidised doctors, engineers, and scientists emigrate, their income taxes accrue to destination governments, while any remittances sent home are untaxed directly and primarily enter the domestic tax base via indirect consumption taxes (VAT/excises). Consistent with this mechanism, `outputs/tables/table3_subgroup_cates.csv` (line 9) shows that in **Low income** countries ($N = 15, C = 9$), the $+10$-year effect on `Direct Income Tax Share` is weak and highly uncertain ($+0.76$ pp, 95% CrI $[-2.46, +3.54]$, $P = 0.735$), and Figure 4 shows that low-income observations (red points at $<5\%$ enrolment) cluster near zero or negative effects on Direct Income Tax Share. Yet Table 4 (`tab:subgroups` in `paper.tex` lines 338-368) **omits the `Low income` and `Lower middle income` rows entirely**, even though the text on line 371 cites the Lower-Middle Income estimate ($+0.99$ pp)!
   - *Tax enforcement capacity and third-party reporting:* Producing university graduates only expands direct income tax revenue if the tax authority has the administrative enforcement capacity and third-party employer withholding coverage to tax high-earning professionals (Kleven et al., 2016; Pomeranz and Vila-Belda, 2019; Okunogbe and Pouliquen, 2022). Self-employed university graduates (physicians, lawyers, accountants, consultants) routinely exhibit high evasion rates where administrative capacity is weak. Remarkably, Section 4.1 (`paper.tex` line 188) claims that $X_{i,t}$ controls for *"external debt, institutional democracy"*, and `data/raw/PROVENANCE.md` (lines 29-30) lists Worldwide Governance Indicators `GE.EST` (Government Effectiveness) and `RL.EST` (Rule of Law), yet inspection of `base_con_vars` and `base_mod_vars` in `src/r/04_run_mvbcf_tax_models.R` (lines 94-126) reveals that **neither external debt, nor institutional democracy, nor WGI Government Effectiveness / Rule of Law is actually included in the models!**
   
   *Actionable fix:* (a) Print the complete set of rows from `table3_subgroup_cates.csv` in Table 4 (including `Low income`, `Lower middle income`, and all five OECD financing regimes, rather than cherry-picking only `OECD Nordic High-Public`), and explain why `OECD Nordic High-Public` exhibits a **negative** $-0.80$ pp effect ($P = 0.03$) on `Non-Resource Tax / GDP`; (b) correct line 188 of `paper.tex` so the text does not claim covariates (`external debt`, `institutional democracy`) that are absent from `base_con_vars`; and (c) incorporate WGI Government Effectiveness / Rule of Law (already downloaded in `data/raw/wb_unesco_imf_wgi_long.csv`) and net migration / skilled emigration proxies as moderators or controls to test how state enforcement capacity and brain drain condition the fiscal return.

5. **Post-treatment collider bias in Model `M2`, unexplained suppression between Total (`M1`) and Direct (`M2`) effects, and overstated "self-financing within a decade" policy conclusions.**  
   To estimate the Mediator-Removed Direct Effect (`M2`), the author appends four contemporaneous 10-year change variables (`d_t2_log_gdp_pc_pct`, `d_t2_tert_enrol_pp`, `d_t2_gini_disp`, `d_t2_life_exp_yrs`) directly into the prognostic forest $\mu(X_{i,t}, M_{i,t+h}, \hat{\pi}(X_{i,t}))$. Simply conditioning on post-treatment realisations $M_{i,t+h}$ inside the outcome regression - rather than implementing sequential g-estimation (Acharya et al., 2016) where the mediator effect is demediated before estimating the baseline prognostic surface - is vulnerable to post-treatment collider bias whenever unobserved macro-fiscal shocks over $[t, t+2]$ jointly affect GDP growth ($M_{i,t+2}$) and tax revenue ($\Delta Y_{i,t+2}$). Notably, in Table 2 (`+10y`), the Total Fiscal Effect (`M1`) on `Non-Resource Tax / GDP` is $+0.37$ pp with a 95% CrI of $[-0.46, +1.14]$ ($P = 0.83$), whereas conditioning on the post-treatment mediators (`M2`) *increases* the point estimate by 43% to $+0.53$ pp ($P = 0.93$, 95% CrI $[-0.16, +1.23]$). If university spending boosts GDP per person (Valero and Van Reenen, 2019) and GDP per person growth boosts tax capacity, removing that positive pathway should make the direct effect *smaller* than the total effect, not larger! The manuscript never explains which of the four mediators acts as a negative suppressor (likely `d_t2_tert_enrol_pp`, because holding enrolment expansion fixed isolates per-student quality from rapid headcount dilution).
   
   Furthermore, the policy takeaway in Section 6.1 (`paper.tex` line 551) - that because a regime shift costs $0.15$ to $0.35$ pp of GDP and raises non-resource tax revenue by $+0.53$ to $+1.06$ pp of GDP at $+10$ years, *"per-student university investments in emerging and massifying economies more than pay for themselves within a decade"* - overstates the empirical evidence:
   - First, a finance minister cares about the **total** fiscal return (`M1`, $+0.37$ pp, 95% CrI $[-0.46, +1.14]$) and the within-country fixed-effects return (`R3b` at $+10$ years in `table4_robustness_checks.csv`: $+0.41$ pp on Non-Resource Tax / GDP with $P = 0.896$, $+0.15$ pp on Direct Income Tax / GDP with $P = 0.814$, and $+0.80$ pp on Direct Income Tax Share with $P = 0.875$, all of which have 95% CrIs spanning zero and was omitted from Table 7!).
   - Second, sustaining a $+0.25$ pp of GDP increase in tertiary spending across a five-to-ten-year wave is a **recurring annual flow cost** (totalling $1.25\%$ to $2.50\%$ of a single year's GDP over 5 to 10 years before discounting and deadweight cost of funds), whereas a $+0.37$ to $+0.53$ pp increase in $\text{Tax/GDP}$ that only materialises between years 5 and 10 does not recoup the cumulative upfront 10-year outlay within the first decade, even if it pays for itself over a 20-to-30-year graduate working career (Hendren and Sprung-Keyser, 2020; Trostel, 2010).
   
   *Actionable fix:* (a) Add a mediator-by-mediator decomposition table showing which of the four post-treatment mediators drives the shift from $+0.37$ pp (`M1`) to $+0.53$ pp (`M2`), and verify `M2` using two-stage sequential g-estimation (Acharya et al., 2016); (b) include the $+10$-year within-country demeaned fixed-effects row (`R3b`) directly in Table 7; and (c) replace the claim that tertiary investments *"more than pay for themselves within a decade"* with an explicit discounted cumulative fiscal flow calculation showing the break-even horizon (typically 12 to 20 years once recurring instructional costs and discounting are counted).
