### 1. Overall Assessment of the Continuous-Treatment Redesign

The redesign of the manuscript from a binary regime cutoff ($Z_{i,t} \in \{0, 1\}$) to a continuous-treatment Multivariate Bayesian Causal Forest ($Z_{i,t} \in [0, 3.0]$) across an expanded 121-country panel (371 country-waves at $+5$ years and 283 country-waves at $+10$ years), validated on a locked 20% country-blocked holdout set (Fold 5, 22 unseen countries at $+10$ years), is a major substantive and methodological step forward. In empirical public finance, national budgets do not operate as binary on/off switches: a binary median threshold artificially implies that a country sitting $0.31\text{ pp}$ below its income-group median (such as New Zealand at $26.1\%$ versus the $26.4\%$ high-income median) reaps the same discrete fiscal dividend from a token spending nudge as a country undertaking a major structural reform. Estimating the partial derivative $\tau(W_{i,t}) = \partial \mathbb{E}[\Delta Y_{i,t+h} \mid X_{i,t}, Z_{i,t}] / \partial Z_{i,t}$ after orthogonalising the continuous dose against random forest expected-treatment predictions $\hat{z}(X_{i,t}) = \mathbb{E}[Z_{i,t} \mid X_{i,t}]$ produces realistic, policy-relevant marginal slopes that scale smoothly with the size of the fiscal commitment.

From a higher-education finance and tax-capacity perspective, the core empirical findings are internally consistent, economically realistic, and stated with appropriate caution. The paper demonstrates that:
1. Higher per-student university funding modernises the tax mix toward direct income, profit, and capital gains taxation over both $+5$ years ($+0.36\text{ pp}$ per $+10\text{ pp}$ dose, $P = 0.93$) and $+10$ years ($+0.71\text{ pp}$, $P = 0.96$), while reducing relative reliance on indirect goods and services taxation ($-0.42\text{ pp}$ at $+10$ years, $P = 0.14$, and $-0.65\text{ pp}$, $P = 0.04$ for the total effect).
2. Overall tax collection as a share of GDP exhibits a temporary "student lock-in" dip at $+5$ years ($-0.06\text{ pp}$ of GDP, $P = 0.31$) while students remain in lecture theatres, and requires a full 10-year horizon (years 10 to 14 after wave start) to turn positive as two cohorts graduate and enter formal salaried careers ($+0.24\text{ pp}$ of GDP on total non-resource tax, $P = 0.89$, and $+0.12\text{ pp}$ of GDP on direct income tax, $P = 0.90$; rising to $+0.30\text{ pp}$, $P = 0.95$, and $+0.16\text{ pp}$, $P = 0.96$ when the macroeconomic growth channel is included).
3. Unbundling the UNESCO denominator proves that real instructional resources per student drive the fiscal return: measuring spending in real 2021 PPP dollars per student ($+25\%$ log step, removing GDP from the denominator) yields $+0.22\text{ pp}$ on total tax/GDP ($P = 0.95$) and $+0.15\text{ pp}$ on direct income tax/GDP ($P = 0.98$), and within-country 5-year per-student expansions ($+10\text{ pp}$) yield $+0.37\text{ pp}$ on direct income tax/GDP ($P = 1.00$) and $+1.56\text{ pp}$ on the direct income tax share ($P = 0.99$), whereas expanding the aggregate university budget share of GDP without accounting for student numbers ($+0.25\text{ pp}$ of GDP) yields near-zero level returns ($-0.06\text{ pp}$ and $-0.04\text{ pp}$).

### 2. Detailed Audit of the Three Domain Review Dimensions

#### 2.1 Realism and Motivation of the Continuous Treatment Scalings
- **Scaling A (Primary linear dose: per $+10\text{ pp}$ of GDP per person per student, centred on training-fold World Bank income-group medians and winsorised at $\pm 1.5$ units / $\pm 15\text{ pp}$):** Well-motivated and directly interpretable for finance ministries. Because the UNESCO ratio equals $\text{University Budget (\% GDP)} \div (\text{Students / Population})$, a $+10\text{ pp}$ change in the per-student ratio multiplies directly by the tertiary student population share to yield the exact national budget cost as a percentage of GDP ($\Delta \text{Budget/GDP} = 0.10 \times \text{Students/Population}$). Across the 44 below-median countries in `2015-2019` (Table 8 / `tab:z0_countries`), a full $+10\text{ pp}$ dose costs a median of $0.40\%$ of GDP per year ($0.29\%$ to $0.81\%$ of GDP in most high-income systems), while a moderate $+2.5\text{ to }+5.0\text{ pp}$ policy reform ($0.25\text{ to }0.50$ dose units) costs $0.10\%$ to $0.20\%$ of GDP. Centring within each World Bank income group and winsorising at $\pm 15\text{ pp}$ prevents the extreme elite-enrolment ratios in low-income countries (raw median $205\%$ at $2.3\%$ enrolment) from dominating tree splits or linear leaf slopes.
- **Scaling B (Relative logarithmic dose: per $+25\%$ log increase around the income-group median, Appendix D Table 11 & Figure A2):** An essential and well-executed complement to Scaling A. In high- and upper-middle-income countries (medians $26.4\%$ and $27.6\%$), a $+25\%$ relative boost is $+6.6\text{ to }+6.9\text{ pp}$ of GDP per person, whereas in lower-middle- and low-income countries (medians $68.9\%$ and $183.8\%$), a $+25\%$ boost corresponds to $+17.2\text{ pp}$ and $+45.9\text{ pp}$ and naturally compresses the skewed right tail of the UNESCO ratio. Showing in Appendix D (`tab:scaling_ab`) that Scaling B sharpens $+10$-year posterior precision on Total Non-Resource Tax/GDP ($+0.26\text{ pp}$, $\text{SD} = 0.154$, $P = 0.95$ vs $+0.24\text{ pp}$, $\text{SD} = 0.200$, $P = 0.89$ under Scaling A) and achieves virtually identical out-of-sample CRPS on the 22 unseen holdout countries ($1.68\text{ vs }1.73\text{ pp}$ on Total Tax/GDP and $0.83\text{ vs }0.86\text{ pp}$ on Direct Income Tax/GDP) reassures readers that the results do not hinge on linear versus logarithmic functional form.
- **Unbundled Continuous Exposures (`M3b` Within-Country 5y Change per $+10\text{ pp}$, `M3d` Real 2021 PPP Dollars per Student per $+25\%$ log, and `M3f` Aggregate University Budget % of GDP per $+0.25\text{ pp}$):** Each step size corresponds to roughly $0.7\text{ to }1.3$ standard deviations of the underlying policy variable ($\text{SD}(Z) = 0.70, 1.33, \text{ and } 1.09$, compared with $1.05$ for Scaling A), making the slopes in Table 3 (`tab:horserace`) and Figure 3 (`fig:horserace`) directly comparable in policy magnitude.

#### 2.2 Fiscal Outcomes, Subgroup Moderation, Cohort Trajectories, and Table B.4 (`tab:z0_countries`)
- **Contrasting Gradients in Subgroup Moderation (Figure 4 in `paper.tex` / Figure 5 in `paper.md` and Table 5 `tab:subgroups`):** The distinction between tax-level mobilisation and tax-mix modernisation across development stages is one of the strongest domain insights in the revised paper. Aggregate tax-to-GDP gains per $+10\text{ pp}$ dose are largest in developing systems that are still building their formal tax bases (`Low Income`: $+0.48\text{ pp}$ Total Tax/GDP, $P = 0.91$; `Lower Middle Income`: $+0.32\text{ pp}$, $P = 0.93$; `Elite Enrolment <20%`: $+0.34\text{ pp}$, $P = 0.93$) and taper off in mature `High Income` economies ($+0.19\text{ pp}$, $P = 0.82$). Conversely, the compositional shift toward direct income taxation (`Direct Income Tax Share`) requires formal third-party reporting firms to absorb graduates and is therefore small in `Low Income` economies ($+0.16\text{ pp}$, $P = 0.69$) but strong across `Lower Middle` ($+0.74\text{ pp}$, $P = 0.96$), `Upper Middle` ($+0.84\text{ pp}$, 95% CI $[+0.09, +1.57]$, $P = 0.98$), `High Income` ($+0.68\text{ pp}$, $P = 0.94$), and all five `OECD Financing Regimes` ($+1.05\text{ to }+1.14\text{ pp}$, $P = 0.93\text{--}0.95$).
- **Cohort Maturation Trajectories from $+5\text{y}$ to $+10\text{y}$ (Figure 5 in `paper.tex` / Figure 6 in `paper.md`):** The trajectory plots clearly communicate both the short-run student lock-in effect on Total Tax/GDP at $+5$ years across all four income groups and the divergence at $+10$ years between middle/high-income economies (where the direct income tax share slope doubles as graduates advance into formal salaried brackets) and low-income economies (where formal-sector scarcity and skilled emigration flatten the compositional slope).
- **Country-Level Cost-Return Translations in Table 8 (`tab:z0_countries` / `tableA4_latest_z0_countries_costs_and_returns.csv`):** Reporting both the cost/return of closing each country's specific gap to its income-group median and the cost/return of a standardised $+10\text{ pp}$ dose across all 44 below-median countries in `2015-2019` directly solves the boundary-cliff criticism of binary models. Marking the six near-boundary countries within $2.5\text{ pp}$ of the median (`New Zealand`, `Iceland`, `Uruguay`, `Romania`, `Italy`, and `Slovenia`) with an asterisk and showing that a tiny gap has a proportionally tiny cost and return is transparent and helpful for country analysts.

### 4. Minor Concerns and Constructive Refinements

1. **Clarifying the Winsorisation Cap on "Gap Returns" vs Uncapped "Gap Costs" for Deep-Gap Developing Countries in Table 8 (`tab:z0_countries`):**
   - **Observation:** In `outputs/tables/tableA4_latest_z0_countries_costs_and_returns.csv` and Table 8 (`paper.tex` lines 615-721), for the 14 countries sitting more than $15\text{ pp}$ below their income-group median (`El Salvador` at $+16.4\text{ pp}$ gap down to `Bangladesh` at $+39.0\text{ pp}$, `Kyrgyz Republic` at $+64.0\text{ pp}$, and `Rwanda` at $+81.0\text{ pp}$), `dose_units_to_median` is capped at $1.5$ units because the model's continuous dose $Z_{i,t} \in [0, 3.0]$ is winsorised at $-1.5$ units ($-15\text{ pp}$) below the group median. Consequently, in Column 5 (`+10y Annual Inc. Tax Return: Gap`), the reported "Gap Return" for those 14 countries is capped at $1.5 \times$ the $+10\text{ pp}$ return (for example, in Bangladesh, $\text{\texteuro}3.03\text{B}$ for the gap vs $\text{\texteuro}2.02\text{B}$ for $+10\text{ pp}$; in Rwanda, $\text{\texteuro}0.12\text{B}$ vs $\text{\texteuro}0.08\text{B}$), whereas Column 3 (`Annual Cost: Gap / +10 pp`) reports the *uncapped* cost of closing the entire $+39.0\text{ pp}$ or $+81.0\text{ pp}$ gap ($\text{\texteuro}8.99\text{B}$ vs $\text{\texteuro}2.31\text{B}$ in Bangladesh; $\text{\texteuro}0.20\text{B}$ vs $\text{\texteuro}0.02\text{B}$ in Rwanda).
   - **Actionable Fix:** Add one brief clarifying sentence in the caption or text of Table 8 (`paper.tex` line 613/617) noting that for countries sitting more than $15\text{ pp}$ below their income-group median ($Z_{i,t} = 0$, rows from El Salvador to Rwanda), the reported gap return in Column 5 is conservatively capped at the model's $+15\text{ pp}$ ($+1.5$ dose unit) winsorisation boundary rather than extrapolated linearly across a $+39\text{ to }+81\text{ pp}$ jump, and that policy makers in those countries should compare the standardised $+10\text{ pp}$ cost and $+10\text{ pp}$ return columns directly.

2. **Two-Cohort 10-Year Flow vs 40-Year Steady-State Workforce Stock in Section 6.1:**
   - **Observation:** In Section 6.1 (`paper.tex` line 335), the text notes that an upfront $+10\text{ pp}$ per-student funding boost costs a median of $0.40\%$ of GDP per year and generates $+0.12\text{ to }+0.37\text{ pp}$ of GDP per year in direct income tax (and $+0.22\text{ to }+0.30\text{ pp}$ of GDP in total non-resource tax) after 10 years, adding that formal net fiscal accounting requires discounting over complete graduate careers. A sceptical finance-ministry reader might initially wonder whether a $+0.12\text{ to }+0.30\text{ pp}$ annual return at year 10 against a $0.40\%$ of GDP annual cost means university spending only partially pays for itself.
   - **Actionable Fix:** Add a short clause in Section 6.1 (`paper.tex` line 335 and `paper.md` line 184) pointing out that at the $+10$-year mark (years 10 to 14), only *two five-year cohorts* of university graduates have entered the workforce and are still in their late twenties or early thirties, whereas in a long-run steady state where 35 to 40 graduate cohorts are simultaneously active across their prime earning years (ages 30 to 60), the cumulative annual tax return across the entire graduate stock is a multiple of the 10-year two-cohort flow.

3. **Outlier Country CATEs on Direct Income Tax Share in Table 8 (`Singapore` and `Macao SAR, China`):**
   - **Observation:** In Table 8 (`tab:z0_countries`, lines 643 and 651), almost every high- and upper-middle-income country has an estimated $+10$-year slope on Direct Income Tax Share of $+0.71\text{ to }+0.97\text{ pp}$, except `Singapore` ($-1.07\text{ pp}$, 95% CI $[-4.16, +2.09]$, while its Direct Income Tax/GDP slope is very large at $+0.61\text{ pp}$) and `Macao SAR, China` ($-0.19\text{ pp}$, 95% CI $[-3.00, +2.61]$, with Direct Income Tax/GDP at $+0.38\text{ pp}$). Both are city-states with unique fiscal architectures (Macao's gaming excise revenues and Singapore's high baseline corporate/personal income tax share and sovereign investment income).
   - **Actionable Fix:** Mentioning in a brief parenthetical in Section 6.2 or the Table 8 text that city-states with atypical non-tax or gaming revenue structures (Singapore and Macao SAR) exhibit wide credible intervals on compositional revenue shares shows close attention to country-specific fiscal detail.
