# Phase 6 Independent Journal Referee Report (Reviewer 4)

**Manuscript Title:** *Does Increasing Government Spending on Tertiary Education Improve the Future Tax Take? A Bayesian Causal Inference Analysis*
**Files Reviewed:** `paper/paper.tex`, `paper/paper.md`, `paper/references.bib`, `outputs/figures/*.png`, `outputs/tables/*.csv`, and `src/`.

### 1. Overall impression

This paper investigates a classic and policy-relevant question in public finance and the economics of education: whether sustained public investment in tertiary education per student expands a country's sovereign non-resource tax revenue ten years later, or shifts the composition of revenue towards direct income, profits, and capital gains taxation. To address the macro-panel challenges of small sample sizes ($N = 283$ country-periods across 112 countries at the 10-year horizon), correlated fiscal outcomes, and strong institutional confounding, the author extends the Multivariate Bayesian Causal Forest (MV-BCF) framework to accommodate a continuous treatment dose ($Z_{i,t}$, scaled per $+10$ percentage points of tertiary spending per student relative to GDP per capita), conditioning the prognostic forest on a cross-fitted random forest expected-treatment score $\hat{z}(X_{i,t}) = \mathbb{E}[Z_{i,t} \mid X_{i,t}]$. The move away from an arbitrary binary median split to a continuous dose-response surface, combined with a four-way denominator comparison, a locked 20% unseen-country holdout validation protocol (Fold 5, 22 unseen countries), and a comparison between additive ratio scaling (`Scaling A`) and multiplicative log scaling (`Scaling B`), represents a major methodological and substantive improvement. The central empirical finding - that a $+10$ percentage point increase in per-student tertiary spending produces a shift in tax composition towards direct income taxes ($+0.71$ percentage points of total taxes, $P(\bar{\tau} > 0 \mid \text{data}) = 0.96$) and a gain in direct income tax over GDP ($+0.12$ to $+0.37$ percentage points, $P = 0.90$ to $1.00$) over a 10-year horizon, whereas aggregate Tertiary Expenditure / GDP without student headcount protection is null - is nuanced, disciplined, and well grounded in fiscal capacity theory.

Before the manuscript is ready for final publication in *Journal of the Royal Statistical Society: Series A* or *Economics of Education Review*, however, three important methodological, reporting, and cross-file synchronisation issues should be resolved. First, there is a discrepancy between the winsorised training-set centring medians used in `02_build_clean_tax_panel.R` (`150.0%`, `71.1%`, `27.9%`, `26.4%`) and the raw training-set medians stated in `paper/paper.tex` (`183.8%`, `68.9%`, `27.6%`, `26.4%`) and `tableA4_latest_z0_countries_costs_and_returns.csv`, alongside a need to clarify the roles of the 80% training split ($N = 229$, `R13`) versus the full 5-fold sample ($N = 283$) in Table 2. Second, the paper benefits from a transparent discussion of why cross-fitted Double Machine Learning (`B6`) and Two-Way Fixed Effects (`B5`) yield smaller point estimates on direct income tax share ($+0.07$ percentage points in DML, which weights by residual treatment variance $\tilde{Z}_{i,t}^2$ and uses linear ridge regression for the outcome nuisance model), and from noting clearly that in out-of-sample 5-fold CV, MV-BCF (`CRPS = 2.33 pp`) matches `Single-Forest BART` (`2.33 pp`) and `Random Forest` (`2.35 pp`) whilst outperforming parametric linear baselines. Third, in Section 5.3 and Figure 6 (`fig5_longitudinal_country_trajectories.pdf`), the caption should state explicitly that the $+5$-year and $+10$-year points come from the horizon-specific MV-BCF models ($N = 371$ and $N = 283$) rather than a joint multi-wave state-space model. My recommendation is **Minor Revision** for *JRSS Series A* / *Economics of Education Review* (and immediate readiness for *arXiv* once these adjustments and the `paper.md` synchronisation are applied).

### 2. Major concerns

1. **Consistent reporting of the continuous treatment centring medians (`med_spend_winsor` vs `med_spend_raw`) across `paper.tex`, `paper.md`, and `tableA4`, and clarifying the role of the 80% training split vs the full 5-fold sample.**
   In `src/r/02_build_clean_tax_panel.R` (lines 290-335), `Z_cont_10pp` (`Scaling A`) is constructed from the winsorised per-student spending series using the training-fold winsorised medians (`150.0%` for low-income, `71.1%` for lower-middle-income, `27.9%` for upper-middle-income, and `26.4%` for high-income countries), whereas `Z_cont_log25pct` (`Scaling B`) is constructed from the raw log spending series using the raw training-fold medians (`183.8%`, `68.9%`, `27.6%`, and `26.4%`). In `paper/paper.tex` (line 167) and `06_generate_figures_and_diagnostics.R` (`tableA4`), the raw medians were cited for `Scaling A`. Both manuscripts and `tableA4` should report and use the exact winsorised training medians (`150.0%`, `71.1%`, `27.9%`, `26.4%`) for `Scaling A` (while noting the raw training medians `183.8%`, `68.9%`, `27.6%`, `26.4%` for `Scaling B`), and Section 3.1 should state explicitly that Table 2 reports the full 5-fold cross-fitted posterior ($N = 283$, 112 countries) whilst the Folds 1 to 4 training-only fit ($N = 229$, 90 countries, `R13` in Appendix Table 7) is used to generate the locked Fold 5 holdout predictions in Section 5.4 and yields even stronger level slopes ($+0.42\text{ pp}$, $P = 0.99$ on Total Tax/GDP and $+0.22\text{ pp}$, $P = 0.98$ on Direct Income Tax/GDP, alongside $+0.64\text{ pp}$, $P = 0.92$ on Direct Income Tax Share).

2. **Explaining the weighting and functional-form differences between MV-BCF and DML (`B6`) in Appendix B, and precision on out-of-sample CRPS comparisons in Section 5.4.**
   In Appendix Table 6 (`\ref{tab:benchmarks}`), Cross-Fitted Double Machine Learning (`B6`) estimates $+0.41\text{ pp}$ on Total Tax/GDP ($P_{\text{freq}} = 0.91$) and $+0.06\text{ pp}$ on Direct Income Tax/GDP ($P_{\text{freq}} = 0.69$), but only $+0.07\text{ pp}$ ($P_{\text{freq}} = 0.56$) on Direct Income Tax Share. Adding two sentences in Appendix B explaining that linear DML (`B6`) uses ridge regression for the outcome model and estimates a variance-weighted overlap slope $\mathbb{E}[\tilde{Z}_{i,t}^2 \tau(X_{i,t})] / \mathbb{E}[\tilde{Z}_{i,t}^2]$ (which heavily weights volatile lower-income country-waves where $\tilde{Z}_{i,t}^2$ is large and where the compositional tax-share slope is smallest, $+0.16\text{ pp}$), whereas MV-BCF estimates the unweighted sample-average slope $\bar{\tau} = N^{-1}\sum_i \tau(W_{i,t})$, provides a clear, insightful econometric explanation. In Section 5.4, the prose already notes that MV-BCF (`2.33 pp`) outperforms parametric and random-walk baselines whilst matching nonparametric random forests (`2.35 pp`) and single-forest BART (`2.33 pp`).

3. **Explicitly stating horizon-specific MV-BCF estimation in the caption of Figure 6 (`fig5_longitudinal_country_trajectories.pdf`) and discussing linear first-stage demediation in `R9`.**
   In Section 5.3 and the caption of Figure 6 (`\ref{fig:trajectories}`), clarify that the $+5$-year and $+10$-year slopes come from the horizon-specific MV-BCF models ($N = 371$ and $N = 283$) rather than a joint multi-wave state-space model, and note in Appendix B that the two-stage sequential g-estimation check (`R9`) uses a linear additive first-stage demediation regression.
