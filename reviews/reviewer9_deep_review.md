# HIGH LEVEL SUMMARY

## Summary
This paper investigates whether national government expenditure on tertiary education strengthens a country's future tax capacity and modernises its tax mix net of general macroeconomic growth. Assembling a 40-year, 5-year-wave panel across 121 countries (1985-2024), the author extends Multivariate Bayesian Causal Forests (MV-BCF) to continuous treatments by incorporating out-of-fold random forest predictions of expected funding into the prognostic tree ensemble while jointly modelling four correlated fiscal outcomes ($Y_1$ to $Y_4$) via a conjugate Inverse-Wishart covariance structure. By decomposing the standard UNESCO per-student spending ratio into four distinct continuous funding measures ($Z_1$ to $Z_4$), the study resolves the "UNESCO denominator trap" and demonstrates that protecting real per-student instructional resources drives a 10-year structural shift toward direct income, profit, and capital gains taxation, whereas expanding aggregate university budgets without accounting for student headcount yields near-zero fiscal returns.

## Strengths
* **Methodological Extension and Empirical Rigour for Moderate Macro Panels:** Extending Multivariate Bayesian Causal Forests to continuous policy treatments via country-blocked cross-fitted treatment orthogonalisation and a joint $4 \times 4$ Inverse-Wishart residual covariance structure effectively borrows strength across strongly correlated fiscal innovations, mitigating regularisation-induced confounding and improving posterior precision over univariate tree models.
* **Bridging Microeconomic Lifecycle Returns and Macroeconomic Tax Capacity:** The study connects single-country microeconomic research on higher education returns with the cross-country political economy of tax enforcement, providing global macro-fiscal evidence that upstream per-student university investments shift national revenue composition toward third-party-enforced direct income taxation over a 10-year cohort maturation window.
* **Unbundling the "UNESCO Denominator Trap" and Institutional Crowding:** Decomposing the standard UNESCO per-student spending-to-GDP-per-capita ratio into income-group-centred ratios ($Z_1$), within-country 5-year expansions ($Z_2$), log real PPP dollars per student ($Z_3$), and aggregate GDP budget shares ($Z_4$) cleanly separates real instructional resources from enrolment headcount and GDP denominator distortions, translating microeconomic findings on institutional crowding to a global panel.
* **Comprehensive Validation, Sensitivity Testing, and Reproducibility:** The empirical analysis is supported by 5-fold country-blocked cross-validation, a locked 20% unseen-country holdout test set, 16 targeted sensitivity and falsification checks (including pre-treatment placebos, within-country fixed-effect demeaning, and non-overlapping decadal waves), and an open-source C++/R reproduction pipeline.

## Weaknesses
* **Direct Conditioning on Post-Treatment Mediators and Unmodelled Longitudinal Error Dependence:** The primary specification includes four post-treatment macroeconomic trajectories directly inside the nonparametric prognostic forest $\mu(\cdot)$ and assumes i.i.d. $\mathcal{N}_4(0, \Sigma)$ errors across repeated and overlapping country-waves ($t \to t+2$). For an applied statistics audience at *The Annals of Applied Statistics*, please consider formally defining the target direct vs. total potential-outcomes estimands, explicitly stating the sequential ignorability assumptions required to avoid collider-stratification bias when conditioning on intermediate variables compared to two-stage sequential g-estimation, and discussing how within-country clustering and moving-average horizon overlap affect posterior credible interval widths (Reference Paper: Explaining causal findings without bias: Detecting and assessing direct effects, Avidit Acharya, Matthew Blackwell, and Maya Sen, 2016; Reference Paper: Estimation and inference of impulse responses by local projections, Òscar Jordà, 2005).
* **Heuristic Resource-Rent Deduction and Central Government WDI Series vs. UNU-WIDER GRD:** Although the paper cites the ICTD / UNU-WIDER Government Revenue Dataset (GRD) to motivate separating volatile commodity royalties from domestic tax effort, the primary outcome pipeline relies on World Bank WDI central government series paired with an ad-hoc rule subtracting half of natural resource rents above 10% of GDP, which also understates state and provincial income taxes in federal countries. The author might consider directly incorporating or benchmarking against the accounting-verified non-resource and consolidated general government tax series from the UNU-WIDER GRD, and clarifying how the resource-rent deduction propagates across the disaggregated outcomes $Y_2$ to $Y_4$ (Reference Paper: Reassessing tax and development research: A new dataset, new findings, and lessons for research, Wilson Prichard, 2016; Reference Paper: UNU-WIDER government revenue dataset, version 2023, UNU-WIDER, 2023).
* **Omission of Frequentist Causal Forests (`grf`) from the Causal Estimator Benchmarks:** While the manuscript positions Bayesian Causal Forests alongside frequentist causal forests in Section 2.3, the causal benchmark suite in Table 3 omits Generalised Random Forests (`grf::causal_forest`) in favour of constant-slope linear panel models and constant-parameter Double ML. Including a country-clustered continuous-treatment frequentist causal forest in Table 3 would provide the most direct frequentist nonparametric tree-ensemble baseline for heterogeneous conditional average partial effects and help explain why constant-slope Double ML yields near-zero estimates on the direct income tax share $Y_3$ (Reference Paper: Generalised random forests, Susan Athey, Julie Tibshirani, and Stefan Wager, 2019; Reference Paper: Double/debiased machine learning for treatment and structural parameters, Victor Chernozhukov et al., 2018).
* **Non-Invariant Prior Variance Scaling Under Shifted Treatment $Z_{i,t} \in [0, 3.0]$ and Treatment Censoring in Low-Income Countries:** Shifting the continuous treatment onto $[0, 3.0]$ rather than centring at zero on $[-1.5, +1.5]$ or using Robinson-style residualisation $\tau(W_{i,t})(Z_{i,t} - \hat{z}_{i,t})$ causes the treatment forest's prior variance contribution $Z_{i,t}^2 M_\tau \Sigma_\tau$ to scale quadratically with funding levels - inducing stronger prior shrinkage in low-spending leaves - while capping the raw UNESCO ratio at 150% prior to centring collapses above-median treatment variation for the low-income stratum to a point mass at $Z_{1,i,t} = 1.5$ (Reference Paper: Bayesian regression tree models for causal inference: Regularisation, confounding, and heterogeneous effects, P. Richard Hahn, Jared S. Murray, and Carlos M. Carvalho, 2020; Reference Paper: Root-N-consistent semiparametric regression, Peter M. Robinson, 1988).
* **Sample Composition Shifts Across $Z_1$-$Z_4$, Moderate Uncertainty on Aggregate Ratios, and Steady-State Extrapolation:** The comparison across funding measures $Z_1$ to $Z_4$ uses sample sizes ranging from $N = 176$ to $N = 403$, which may conflate denominator definitions with country reporting selection; additionally, the primary 95% credible intervals under $Z_1$ for aggregate GDP-denominated outcomes ($Y_1, Y_2$) straddle zero ($P \approx 0.89\text{--}0.90$), and the 40-year multi-cohort payback calculation extrapolates a 10-year macro local projection by a factor of 4 without real discounting. Evaluating $Z_1$-$Z_4$ on a common overlapping sample, tempering causal phrasing where 95% CIs cross zero, increasing MCMC chain lengths so effective sample sizes exceed 400, and framing the 40-year steady-state figures as illustrative back-of-the-envelope benchmarks would strengthen the paper.

1. **Direct Conditioning on Post-Treatment Macroeconomic Mediators Inside the Nonparametric Prognostic Forest $\mu(\cdot)$ (Section 1, Pages 2-3, Figure 1; Section 2.3, Page 5; Section 4, Page 9):**
   - **Potential Concern:** In Section 1 (page 2), Figure 1 (Box 2B, page 3), Section 2.3 (page 5), and Section 4 (page 9), the covariate vector $X_{i,t}$ passed into the 75-tree baseline prognostic ensemble $\mu\big(X_{i,t}, \hat{z}(X_{i,t})\big)$ in Equation (2) combines 19 pre-treatment characteristics measured at or before wave $t$ with four follow-up macroeconomic variables measured over the post-treatment window $[t, t+h]$ (forward GDP per person growth, university enrolment expansion, income inequality changes, and life expectancy changes) to *"isolate the direct structural effect of university spending net of overall economic growth,"* citing Imai et al. (2010), Acharya et al. (2016), VanderWeele (2016), and Ting and Linero (2025). While isolating the direct structural fiscal effect of university funding net of general economic growth is substantively well-motivated, feeding post-treatment intermediate variables directly into an unconstrained nonparametric tree ensemble $\mu(\cdot)$ raises two methodological considerations for a statistical methodology audience such as *Annals of Applied Statistics*:
     1. *Collider / Intermediate Confounder Bias:* As established in the causal mediation literature cited in the text (Imai et al., 2010; Acharya et al., 2016; VanderWeele, 2016), conditioning directly on post-treatment mediators in an outcome regression only identifies a Controlled Direct Effect (CDE) under strong sequential ignorability assumptions (specifically, no unmeasured confounders between the post-treatment macroeconomic trajectories and the tax outcomes $\Delta Y_{i,t+h}$, and no treatment-induced intermediate confounders). If unobserved fiscal, institutional, or productivity shocks during $[t, t+h]$ jointly affect both intermediate growth/enrolment trajectories and future tax collection, conditioning on those post-treatment variables inside $\mu(\cdot)$ opens collider paths and can induce post-treatment selection or collider-stratification bias.
     2. *Unconstrained Treatment-Mediator Tree Interactions and Target Estimand Ambiguity:* Because decision trees in $\mu\big(X_{i,t}, \hat{z}(X_{i,t})\big)$ can form arbitrary multi-way splits combining pre-treatment covariates, the predicted funding level $\hat{z}(X_{i,t})$, and post-treatment mediators, whereas the treatment forest $\tau(W_{i,t}) Z_{i,t}$ splits only on pre-treatment moderators $W_{i,t}$, the prognostic forest can absorb treatment-mediator interactions in an unstructured way. Unlike two-stage sequential g-estimation (Acharya et al., 2016) - which first demediates the outcome and then estimates the treatment effect conditional strictly on pre-treatment covariates - including post-treatment variables directly inside $\mu(\cdot)$ leaves the formal potential-outcomes definition of $\tau(W_{i,t})$ (e.g., Average Controlled Direct Effect vs. Natural Direct Effect) ambiguous unless no-interaction assumptions between treatment and mediators hold.
   - **Suggested Improvement:** Although the robustness checks elsewhere in the manuscript (the total-effect specification M1b omitting follow-up controls in Table 3 and the two-stage sequential g-estimation check R9 in Table 4) substantially mitigate concerns that the empirical conclusions are artifacts of post-treatment conditioning, the methodological exposition across Section 1, Section 2.3, and Section 4 would be strengthened by: (i) notationally separating the 19 pre-treatment baseline confounders $X_{i,t}^{\text{pre}}$ from the 4 post-treatment mediators $M_{i,t \to t+h}$ in Equations (2) and (3) rather than bundling both under the time-$t$ symbol $X_{i,t}$ (which also clarifies in Section 4, Paragraph 4 that $\hat{z}(X_{i,t}^{\text{pre}})$ is estimated exclusively from pre-treatment variables), (ii) formally defining the target potential-outcomes estimands for the total and controlled/natural direct effects, and (iii) explicitly stating the sequential unconfoundedness assumptions required when conditioning on $M_{i,t \to t+h}$ inside $\mu(\cdot)$ while clarifying why single-stage conditioning is used as the primary specification alongside Model M1b and Check R9.

2. **Induced Prior Variance Heteroskedasticity from Shifting $Z_{i,t}$ onto $[0, 3.0]$ vs. Residualised Parameterisations (Section 4, Page 9; Appendix A, Page 18):**
   - **Potential Concern:** In Equations (2) and (3), the centred continuous treatment variable is shifted by $+1.5$ so that $Z_{i,t} \in [0, 3.0]$ is strictly non-negative (with $Z_{i,t} = 1.5$ representing the income-group median), and enters the treatment forest multiplicatively as $Z_{i,t} \sum_{l=1}^{M_\tau} g_\tau(W_{i,t}; T_l^\tau, M_l^\tau)$ with independent zero-mean Gaussian leaf priors $\tau_{l,b} \sim \mathcal{N}_4(0, \Sigma_\tau)$. Unlike standard binary BCF (where coding $Z \in \{-0.5, +0.5\}$ vs. $\{0, 1\}$ is a known prior-regularisation subtlety discussed in Hahn et al., 2020), multiplying a zero-mean Gaussian tree ensemble by an uncentred, non-negative continuous scalar $Z_{i,t} \in [0, 3.0]$ has two statistical consequences:
     1. *Location-Shift Variance Scaling and Asymmetric Shrinkage:* Conditional on the tree structures, the induced marginal prior covariance of the outcome vector given $(X_{i,t}, W_{i,t}, Z_{i,t})$ is:
        $$\operatorname{Var}(\Delta Y_{i,t+h} \mid X_{i,t}, W_{i,t}, Z_{i,t}) = M_\mu \Sigma_\mu + Z_{i,t}^2 M_\tau \Sigma_\tau + \Sigma.$$
        Because $Z_{i,t}$ is shifted onto $[0, 3.0]$ rather than centred at zero on $[-1.5, +1.5]$, the prior variance contributed by the treatment forest increases quadratically with $Z_{i,t}$: it is $0$ at the lower winsorisation boundary ($Z_{i,t} = 0$, or $-15\text{ pp}$ below the median), $2.25 M_\tau \Sigma_\tau$ at the group median ($Z_{i,t} = 1.5$), and $9.0 M_\tau \Sigma_\tau$ at the upper boundary ($Z_{i,t} = 3.0$, or $+15\text{ pp}$ above the median). This forces $\mu\big(X_{i,t}, \hat{z}(X_{i,t})\big)$ to represent the counterfactual baseline trajectory at an extreme funding cut ($Z_{i,t} = 0$) rather than at the benchmark median or expected funding level. Moreover, in the conjugate leaf precision update $\Omega_{\tau,b}^{-1} = \Sigma_\tau^{-1} + \left(\sum_{i \in b} Z_{i,t}^2\right)\Sigma^{-1}$, leaves populated by low-spending countries ($Z_{i,t}$ near $0$) have much smaller $\sum_{i \in b} Z_{i,t}^2$ and thus experience stronger shrinkage toward the prior mean of zero than leaves populated by high-spending countries ($Z_{i,t}$ near $3.0$), even when the local sample size and within-leaf treatment variance are identical, making the regularisation non-invariant to the $+1.5$ location shift.
     2. *Contrast with Robinson (1988) Residualisation and the Generalised Propensity Score:* Section 4 states that replacing the binary propensity score with $\hat{z}(X_{i,t}) = \mathbb{E}[Z_{i,t} \mid X_{i,t}]$ inside $\mu(\cdot)$ ensures that $\tau(W_{i,t})$ is identified from local funding deviations around predicted spending. However, in Robinson's (1988) partially linear model and Generalised Random Forests (Athey et al., 2019), the treatment multiplier itself is residualised as $\tau(W_{i,t}) \cdot \big(Z_{i,t} - \hat{z}(X_{i,t})\big)$, whereas Equations (2)-(3) multiply $\tau(W_{i,t})$ by the uncentred level $Z_{i,t} \in [0, 3.0]$ and rely on the step-function splits of $\mu\big(X_{i,t}, \hat{z}_{i,t}\big)$ to absorb $\tau(W_{i,t})\hat{z}(X_{i,t})$. Furthermore, while $\mathbb{E}[Z \mid X] = P(Z=1 \mid X)$ completely characterises a binary treatment distribution, the Generalised Propensity Score (Hirano and Imbens, 2004) for a continuous treatment depends on the conditional density $f_{Z \mid X}(z \mid x)$; conditioning solely on the conditional first moment $\hat{z}(X_{i,t})$ acts as a mean-sufficiency summary and relies on any higher-order conditional variance (heteroskedasticity) or skewness in $Z_{i,t} \mid X_{i,t}$ being either approximately homogeneous or captured directly by $X_{i,t}$ inside $\mu(\cdot)$.
   - **Suggested Improvement:** Clarify in Section 4 and Appendix A why $Z_{i,t}$ was shifted onto $[0, 3.0]$ rather than centred at the income-group median ($[-1.5, +1.5]$) or parameterised via Robinson-style residualisation $\tau(W_{i,t}) \cdot \big(Z_{i,t} - \hat{z}(X_{i,t})\big)$, discuss how the prior variance scaling across $[0, 3.0]$ affects leaf regularisation, and note the mean-sufficiency assumption underlying $\hat{z}(X_{i,t})$.

3. **Unmodelled Within-Country Serial Correlation and Overlapping Horizon Dependence Under the i.i.d. $\mathcal{N}_4(0, \Sigma)$ Error Assumption (Section 4, Page 9; Appendix A, Page 18):**
   - **Potential Concern:** Equations (2) and (3) specify the error vectors as independent and identically distributed across all pooled country-wave observations: $\varepsilon_{i,t+h} \stackrel{\text{i.i.d.}}{\sim} \mathcal{N}_4(0, \Sigma)$. In this longitudinal macro-panel, there are two sources of within-country dependence omitted from the working likelihood:
     1. *Within-Country Cluster Dependence:* Each country contributes multiple waves ($N = 371$ across 121 countries at $h=1$, and $N = 283$ across 112 countries at $h=2$, averaging roughly 2.5 to 3.1 waves per country). Persistent, unobserved country-level fiscal dynamics not absorbed by $X_{i,t}$ induce positive residual correlation across waves of the same country.
     2. *Overlapping 10-Year Difference Windows at $h = 2$:* Because waves are constructed in 5-year intervals ($t, t+1, t+2$), at the primary $+10$-year horizon ($h = 2$) consecutive two-period outcome changes $\Delta Y_{i,t \to t+2}$ and $\Delta Y_{i,t+1 \to t+3}$ for the same country share the intermediate 5-year period $[t+1, t+2]$. Standard local-projection theory (Jordà, 2005) implies that multi-step changes over overlapping intervals mechanically induce a moving-average $\text{MA}(1)$ serial correlation structure in $\varepsilon_{i,t+2}$ within each country even if single-wave innovations are independent. (Consistent with this, Table 7 Row 7 shows that the estimated residual standard deviations increase from $h=1$ to $h=2$.)
     Treating repeated and overlapping country-waves as conditionally independent inside the Gaussian marginal likelihood $L(R \mid T, \Sigma)$ and the Inverse-Wishart update ignores positive intra-country dependence, which can overstate the effective sample size of independent observations and narrow posterior credible intervals.
   - **Suggested Improvement:** While non-overlapping 10-year decadal waves (Check R2) and within-country fixed-effect demeaning (Checks R3a/R3b) are evaluated in Appendix B, Section 4 and Appendix A should explicitly acknowledge around Equations (2)-(3) that the i.i.d. Gaussian working likelihood abstracts from within-country clustering and the mechanical $\text{MA}(1)$ overlap at $h=2$, pointing readers directly to Checks R2 and R3 as empirical sensitivity checks on this dependence structure.

### [Conclusions, Multi-Cohort Payback Arithmetic, Limitations, and Reproducibility] (Pages: 15-18, 27-31)
#### Potential Mistakes and Improvements

1. **Structural Assumptions in the Multi-Cohort Steady-State Payback Extrapolation Beyond the 10-Year Observation Window (Section 5.1, Page 11; Sections 6.1 and 6.2, Pages 16-17; Appendix B, Page 22):**
   - *Potential Concern:* In Section 5.1 (page 11), Section 6.1 (page 16), Section 6.2 (Limitation 5, page 17), and Appendix B (page 22), the text explains why single-year Year-10 tax returns (€0.33B-€0.36B in direct income tax $Y_2$, or €0.55B in $Y_2$ and €1.05B in total tax $Y_1$ at the high-income group mean slope) are smaller than the annual tuition cost (€2.16B-€2.37B) in high-enrolment countries like Ireland. The text argues that at Year 10, only the first two five-year graduate cohorts have entered the workforce, whereas over a 40-year (eight-wave) working career, eight educated cohorts are in the workforce simultaneously per cohort currently in university. In Limitation 5 (page 17), the text multiplies the Year-10 annual gain by 4 (the ratio of 8 active steady-state cohorts to the 2 cohorts graduated by Year 10) to state that accumulating the Year-10 tax gain across a graduate cohort's 40-year career (or across all eight active cohorts in steady state) yields "€1.4 billion to €4.2 billion per year in Ireland."
   - *Substantive Consideration:* While the cohort accounting identity (1 five-year wave in university vs. 8 five-year waves in the labour force) is arithmetically clear, multiplying the estimated 10-year macro-panel change $\Delta Y_{i,t+2}$ by 4 relies on strong structural assumptions that are not directly tested because the empirical panel only observes outcomes up to $t+2$ (+10 years):
     1. **Transitory Pulse vs. Persistent Steady-State Treatment Trajectory:** The empirical model estimates the local projection $\Delta Y_{i,t+2} = Y_{i,t+2} - Y_{i,t}$ as a function of the funding level $Z_{1,i,t}$ in wave $t$, without conditioning on or holding fixed future funding shocks $Z_{1,i,t+1}$ and $Z_{1,i,t+2}$ via sequential dynamic treatment regimes. If $Z_{1,i,t}$ represents an isolated one-wave (5-year) funding pulse for a single cohort, only one treated five-year cohort (not two) has entered the labour market by wave $t+2$ (years 10-14, having worked for 5 to 9 years). Conversely, because national university spending ratios are serially correlated across waves, if $Z_{1,i,t}$ reflects a permanent steady-state funding shift so that all eight active cohorts are educated at the higher standard, the observed change $\Delta Y_{i,t+2}$ already reflects an ongoing multi-wave funding pattern rather than strictly the isolated contribution of two newly treated cohorts, and the €2.37B/yr funding cost is also incurred *every* year in steady state as new cohorts enter university. Because the empirical estimator $\tau(W_{i,t})$ is a macro-level local projection rather than a cohort-specific longitudinal lifecycle estimator, attributing $\Delta Y_{i,t+2}$ strictly to "two graduated cohorts" mixes macro-level impulse responses with micro-cohort accounting.
     2. **Real Discounting and General-Equilibrium Saturation:** Multiplying a Year-10 flow by 4 to represent a 40-year career return applies a 0% real discount rate over several decades (whereas sovereign borrowing costs over a 10-to-40-year horizon are non-trivial) and assumes zero skill depreciation and no general-equilibrium wage compression or diminishing macro-fiscal returns as the stock of university graduates quadruples across eight cohorts.
   - *Actionable Suggestion:* Clarify the distinction between a one-off five-year cohort spending pulse and a permanent steady-state funding shift in Section 5.1, Sections 6.1-6.2, and Appendix B, and explicitly caveat the steady-state multiplication as an *illustrative back-of-the-envelope lifecycle benchmark* rather than a causally identified 40-year impulse response from the model, noting that (a) the panel only observes outcomes up to +10 years, (b) future 40-year cohort returns should be discounted to present value, and (c) macro-level local projections capture aggregate economy-wide adjustments over 10 years rather than tracking individual longitudinal graduate cohorts in isolation.
