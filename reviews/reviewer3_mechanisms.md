# Referee Report: Reviewer 3 (Mechanisms, Accounting Identities & Plausibility Expert)

**Manuscript Title:** *Does Increasing Government Spend on Tertiary Education Improve the Future Tax Take? A Bayesian Causal Inference Analysis*

---

### 1. Overall impression

This manuscript addresses a central question in public finance and the economics of education: whether government expenditure on tertiary education generates a structural future tax dividend above and beyond general macroeconomic growth. The author constructs a 40-year cross-country panel of five-year waves (1985 - 2024) covering up to 121 countries and applies Multivariate Bayesian Causal Forests (`mvbcf`) and a three-wave Longitudinal Bayesian Causal Forest (`lbcf`) to four disaggregated tax outcomes over $+5$-year and $+10$-year horizons. The conceptual framing around the UNESCO per-student denominator trap, the distinction between total effects (`M1`) and mediator-removed direct effects (`M2`), and the open availability of custom C++ (`RcppArmadillo`) and R replication scripts are notable strengths. All references in the bibliography were audited and verified to be real academic works with valid DOIs.

Despite its strong methodological craft and clear writing, the paper currently suffers from six major substantive flaws in its accounting definitions, causal mediation mechanics, cohort-size arithmetic, and longitudinal specification consistency that prevent publication in its present form. Auditing the underlying data-construction and modelling scripts (`src/r/02_build_clean_tax_panel.R`, `src/r/04_run_mvbcf_tax_models.R`, and `src/r/05_run_longitudinal_bcf_tax.R`) against the manuscript text reveals: (1) an undisclosed heuristic construction of non-resource tax revenue and a denominator mismatch in the construction of direct income tax to GDP that mechanically couples Outcomes 1, 2, and 3; (2) an unexplained suppressor/dilution puzzle in which removing post-treatment GDP and enrolment growth (`M2`) yields larger estimates than the total effect (`M1`), while removing GDP from the treatment denominator (`M3d`, real PPP dollars per student) causes the headline direct income tax share effect to drop by more than half to statistical insignificance ($+0.74$ pp, $P = 0.79$); (3) effect-size magnitudes at $+5$ and $+10$ years that are implausibly large for a single five-year graduating cohort (representing only $2.5\%$ to $5\%$ of the labour force) unless driven by persistent multi-decade funding regimes, mechanical payroll withholding on university staff salaries, or within-country level differences; (4) a temporal look-ahead bug and covariate/mediator mismatch in the three-wave longitudinal model (`lbcf`), where Wave 1 conditions on Wave 2 future mediators and where `Direct Income Tax / GDP` actually turns negative at $+5$ years ($-0.06$ pp); (5) single-stage post-treatment conditioning in `M2` that is vulnerable to collider bias under concurrent macro-fiscal shocks; and (6) an unaddressed $+0.58$ pp ($P = 0.854$) pre-treatment placebo drift on direct income tax share alongside a discrepancy between the baseline covariates claimed in the text and those actually coded. My recommendation is **major revision** (or **reject and resubmit** at a top general-interest economics or statistics journal) pending a thorough overhaul of these accounting and identification issues.

### 2. Major concerns

1. **Undisclosed Synthetic Construction of `Non-Resource Tax / GDP`, Denominator Mismatch in `Direct Income Tax / GDP`, and Mechanical Coupling Among Outcomes 1, 2, and 3.**
   In Section 3.1 (line 121) and Section 3.2 (line 130), the manuscript states that non-resource tax revenue is drawn from the UNU-WIDER Government Revenue Dataset (Prichard, 2016; ICTD/UNU-WIDER, 2023) and claims in the leakage audit (`leakage_audit.csv`, Check `L2`) that there are zero mechanical product identities among the outcomes. However, inspecting `src/r/01_download_tax_education_data.R`, `data/raw/PROVENANCE.md`, and `src/r/02_build_clean_tax_panel.R` (lines 199 - 208) shows that no UNU-WIDER GRD dataset is loaded in `data/raw/`. Instead, `non_resource_tax_gdp_pct` ($Y^{(1)}$ in levels) is fabricated via an undisclosed piecewise subtraction from World Bank WDI tax-to-GDP:
   $$\text{NonResTax/GDP}_{i,t} = \max\Bigl(\text{Tax/GDP}_{i,t} - 0.5\max\bigl(\text{ResourceRents/GDP}_{i,t} - 10,\, 0\bigr),\, 2.0\Bigr),$$
   with a secondary fallback to $\max(\text{GovRevExGrants/GDP}_{i,t} - 0.7\cdot\text{ResourceRents/GDP}_{i,t},\, 2.0)$ when WDI tax revenue is missing. Even more seriously, line 206 of `02_build_clean_tax_panel.R` constructs Outcome 2 (`inc_tax_gdp_pct`, $Y^{(2)}$ in levels) by multiplying `non_resource_tax_gdp_pct` ($Y^{(1)}$) by `income_profit_tax_share_rev_pct / 100` ($Y^{(3)} / 100$, WDI code `GC.TAX.YPKG.ZS`). This creates two problems. First, `GC.TAX.YPKG.ZS` is direct income, profit, and capital gains tax as a percentage of **total government revenue** (which includes non-tax revenue, social contributions, and grants), not as a percentage of non-resource tax revenue; multiplying non-resource tax/GDP by income tax/total revenue mixes incompatible denominators and systematically understates direct income tax/GDP whenever non-tax revenue is positive. Second, because $Y^{(2)}_{i,t} \equiv Y^{(1)}_{i,t} \times Y^{(3)}_{i,t} / 100$ by construction, the ten-year changes satisfy the exact accounting identity
   $$\Delta Y^{(2)}_{i,t+h} = \frac{Y^{(3)}_{i,t}}{100}\Delta Y^{(1)}_{i,t+h} + \frac{Y^{(1)}_{i,t}}{100}\Delta Y^{(3)}_{i,t+h} + \frac{\Delta Y^{(1)}_{i,t+h}\Delta Y^{(3)}_{i,t+h}}{100}.$$
   Consequently, Outcome 2 is not an independent fiscal measurement; it is an exact bilinear function of Outcomes 1 and 3, which mechanically generates the $+0.703$ to $+0.712$ residual correlation reported in Table 7.

   *Actionable fix:* First, either merge the actual UNU-WIDER GRD database into `data/raw/` or explicitly write out the piecewise resource-rent adjustment formula in Section 3.2 and remove the claim that UNU-WIDER GRD series are directly ingested. Second, construct `Direct Income Tax / GDP` using the consistent WDI indicator `GC.TAX.YPKG.RV.ZS` (% of total taxes, listed in `PROVENANCE.md` line 17 but unused in `02_build_clean_tax_panel.R`) multiplied by `GC.TAX.TOTL.GD.ZS` (total tax/GDP), or pull direct income tax/GDP directly from IMF GFS / UNU-WIDER GRD. Third, state explicitly in Section 3.2 and Section 5.4 that Outcome 2 is the product of the tax-to-GDP ratio and the income tax share, so part of the precision gain in joint `mvbcf` over univariate `BCF` reflects this deterministic accounting link.

2. **Shared GDP Denominator Dynamics, the `M2 > M1` Mediation Puzzle, and Selective Reporting of the Real PPP Dollars per Student Unbundling (`M3d`).**
   Because GDP per person sits in the denominator of the primary treatment ratio ($\text{UNESCO Ratio}_{i,t} = \frac{\text{TertSpend/Student}_{i,t}}{\text{GDP/pc}_{i,t}} \times 100$) and GDP sits in the denominator of the tax-to-GDP outcomes ($\text{Tax/GDP}_{i,t}$), GDP shocks create subtle denominator effects. Within any World Bank income group, countries with lower baseline GDP per person (or a temporary GDP trough at wave $t$) have a mechanically larger UNESCO ratio ($Z_{i,t} = 1$). Over $[t, t+2]$, if treated countries experience faster GDP growth (either via human capital output effects or mean reversion from a wave-$t$ trough), the growing denominator $\text{GDP}_{i,t+2}$ dilutes $\text{Tax}_{i,t+2}/\text{GDP}_{i,t+2}$ whenever overall tax buoyancy is close to unity. This explains a striking anomaly in Table 2 that the manuscript mentions in a single sentence (line 254) without theoretical explanation: why is the mediator-removed direct effect (`M2`) *larger* than the total effect (`M1`) across both $+5$ and $+10$ years ($+0.53$ pp vs $+0.37$ pp on `Non-Resource Tax / GDP`, and $+1.66$ pp vs $+1.38$ pp on `Direct Income Tax Share`)? If tertiary education expanded fiscal capacity partly *by* raising GDP per person and tertiary enrolment, removing those positive mediating pathways should shrink `M2` relative to `M1`. The fact that `M2 > M1` implies that the post-treatment mediators act as negative suppressors (either because forward GDP growth dilutes the tax/GDP denominator or because countries with high UNESCO ratios within an income tier have slower raw GDP growth).

   Even more importantly, when the author removes GDP per person from the treatment denominator in Table 3 Row 3 (`M3d`, `Real PPP $ / Student`), the manuscript highlights that `Non-Resource Tax / GDP` doubles to $+1.06$ pp ($P = 0.99$), while failing to discuss in the text that the paper's primary headline channel, `Direct Income Tax Share`, collapses from $+1.66$ pp ($P = 0.98$) to **$+0.74$ pp ($P = 0.79$, 95% CrI $[-1.02, +2.55]$)**. Within each income tier, high real PPP spending per student simply picks out the richest countries near the upper threshold of that income bracket. In addition, `real_tert_spend_per_student_ppp` is constructed in `02_build_clean_tax_panel.R` (line 215) by multiplying the nominal-LCU UNESCO ratio by economy-wide constant-2021 PPP GDP per person, which implicitly deflates university spending by the GDP-wide PPP price level rather than a higher-education wage deflator (ignoring Balassa-Samuelson and Baumol cost-disease effects where university faculty salaries scale with domestic GDP per capita).

   *Actionable fix:* Add a dedicated paragraph in Section 5.1 explaining the accounting mechanics of why `M2 > M1` (reporting the first-stage effect of $Z_{i,t}$ on the four mediators $\Delta \log \text{GDP/pc}_{t+h}$, $\Delta \text{Enrol}_{t+h}$, $\Delta \text{Gini}_{t+h}$, and $\Delta \text{LifeExp}_{t+h}$). In Section 5.2, explicitly discuss why `Direct Income Tax Share` becomes noisy and statistically indistinguishable from zero ($+0.74$ pp, $P = 0.79$) under `M3d` (`Real PPP $ / Student`), and report the results for `Z_real_spend_expansion` (within-country $\ge 5\%$ growth in real PPP dollars per student, which is already constructed in `02_build_clean_tax_panel.R` line 269 but omitted from Table 3).

3. **Macroeconomic Effect-Size Plausibility, Back-of-the-Envelope Cohort Arithmetic, and Attenuation Under Within-Country Fixed Effects (`R3b`).**
   A rigorous back-of-the-envelope cohort calculation raises serious questions about whether the reported $+5$-year and $+10$-year effect sizes can be interpreted as the causal human-capital return of a single five-year funding wave. In a typical lower- or upper-middle-income country (Table 1), gross tertiary enrolment is $7.4\%$ to $25.5\%$ of the five-year post-secondary age cohort, and the public tertiary budget is $0.64\%$ to $0.65\%$ of GDP. A single five-year student cohort represents roughly $5 / 40 = 12.5\%$ of the 40-year working-age span; at a $20\%$ tertiary enrolment rate, the treated student cohort entering the workforce over a five-year wave constitutes only $12.5\% \times 20\% = \mathbf{2.5\%}$ **of the national labour force** (or $5\%$ over two waves). Moreover, in Model `M2`, both **forward GDP per person growth** and **forward tertiary enrolment expansion** are held constant. For a cohort representing $2.5\%$ to $5\%$ of the labour force to raise national `Non-Resource Tax / GDP` by $+0.53$ to $+1.06$ percentage points of GDP (and `Direct Income Tax / GDP` by $+0.28$ to $+0.81$ pp of GDP) *without increasing GDP per person or enrolment headcount*, existing national income equal to $2.5\%$ to $5.3\%$ of total GDP would have to shift from untaxed informal activity into formal tax brackets subject to a $20\%$ effective tax rate. In fiscal multiplier terms, since an above-median tertiary spending regime or a $\ge 2.5$ pp per-student expansion costs roughly $0.20$ to $0.35$ pp of GDP (line 551), a $+0.53$ to $+1.06$ pp annual tax-to-GDP increase at year 10 implies an undiscounted single-year recurring tax-back ratio of $1.5\times$ to $5.3\times$ the annual budgetary cost even after stripping out GDP growth.

   Three non-graduate mechanisms likely inflate these numbers: (i) **mechanical PAYE payroll clawback**, whereby higher per-student spending immediately pays higher formal salaries to university professors and administrators, returning $15\%$ to $25\%$ of the spending increment directly to the treasury as income tax withholding within the first five years (explaining why $70\%$ of the ten-year shift in `Direct Income Tax Share` already appears at $+5$ years, $+1.17$ pp vs $+1.66$ pp, when most wave-$t$ students have barely graduated); (ii) **treatment regime persistence**, because `Z_regime_inc` is a cross-sectional level indicator (above/below the income-group median) that is highly serially correlated within countries across decades, so comparing $Z_{i,t} = 1$ against $Z_{i,t} = 0$ captures cumulative multi-decade institutional regimes rather than a single five-year policy shock; and (iii) **cross-sectional level confounding**, as shown by inspecting `outputs/tables/table4_robustness_checks.csv` (lines 14 - 17, specification `R3b_CountryFE_Demean_10y`). When outcomes are demeaned within country at the $+10$-year horizon (`R3b`), the effect on `Direct Income Tax / GDP` falls by nearly half from $+0.28$ pp ($P = 0.92$) to **$+0.15$ pp ($P = 0.81$, 95% CrI $[-0.19, +0.48]$)**, and the effect on `Direct Income Tax Share` drops by more than half from $+1.66$ pp ($P = 0.98$) to **$+0.80$ pp ($P = 0.87$, 95% CrI $[-0.61, +2.05]$)**. Noticeably, Table 6 (`tab:robust`) in the manuscript reports `R3a` ($+5\text{y}$) instead of `R3b` ($+10\text{y}$), even though every other specification in Table 6 is reported at $+10$ years, and line 539 mentions `R3b` only for `Non-Resource Tax / GDP` ($+0.41$ pp) while omitting the sharp $+10$-year attenuation on the two direct income tax channels.

   *Actionable fix:* Add an explicit cohort-accounting and Marginal Value of Public Funds (MVPF) calculation in Section 6.1 that quantifies (a) the labour-force share of a five-year graduating cohort, (b) the mechanical income-tax clawback on university faculty/staff payrolls at $+5$ years, and (c) the transition matrix / serial persistence $\Pr(Z_{i,t+1}=1 \mid Z_{i,t}=1)$ of the binary treatment regime. In addition, include the full `+10y` within-country demeaned row (`R3b`) in Table 6 alongside `R3a`, and candidly discuss the attenuation of the $+10$-year direct income tax estimates when between-country variation is removed.

4. **Temporal Look-Ahead Bug, Covariate Omission, and Conflicting $+5$-Year Estimates in the Three-Wave Longitudinal BCF (`lbcf`).**
   In Section 5.3 (Table 5), the three-wave Longitudinal BCF (`lbcf`) is presented as confirming the `mvbcf` results. However, comparing Table 2 (`mvbcf`) and Table 5 (`lbcf`) on Outcome 2 (`Direct Income Tax / GDP`) reveals a major contradiction: whereas `mvbcf` estimates a significant positive effect at $+5$ years ($+0.20$ pp, $P = 0.94$) and $+10$ years ($+0.28$ pp, $P = 0.92$), `lbcf` estimates a **negative** initial $+5$-year effect ($\tau_1 = -0.06$ pp, $P = 0.36$) and a weak, statistically indistinct cumulative $+10$-year effect ($\tau_{\text{cum}} = +0.14$ pp, $P = 0.79$, 95% CrI $[-0.20, +0.50]$). Likewise, on the negative-control outcome (`Goods & Services Tax Share`), `lbcf` estimates a positive $+5$-year effect of $+0.43$ pp ($P = 0.74$) vs $-0.22$ pp in `mvbcf`.

   Inspecting `src/r/05_run_longitudinal_bcf_tax.R` (lines 74 - 131) uncovers three undocumented specification discrepancies and a temporal leakage bug in `lbcf`:
   - First, in lines 94 - 98 and 123 - 130 of `05_run_longitudinal_bcf_tax.R`, `growth_vars_med` stacks **both** the $+5$-year mediators (`d_t1_log_gdp_pc_pct`, `d_t1_tert_enrol_pp`) and the $+10$-year mediators (`d_t2_log_gdp_pc_pct`, `d_t2_tert_enrol_pp`) into a single time-invariant matrix `X_growth_stack` that is passed into **both** the Wave 1 growth forest (`Z_growth1`, covering $[t, t+1]$) and the Wave 2 growth forest (`Z_growth2`, covering $[t+1, t+2]$). As a result, the Wave 1 prognostic growth forest $\mu_1(\cdot)$ at $+5$ years splits on `d_t2_log_gdp_pc_pct` and `d_t2_tert_enrol_pp`, which are future economic and enrolment changes occurring between years 5 and 10 ($t+1 \to t+2$). Conditioning Wave 1 outcomes on Wave 2 realisations violates temporal causality.
   - Second, `growth_vars_med` in `05_run_longitudinal_bcf_tax.R` omits two of the four mediators used in `M2` (`d_t1_gini_disp`/`d_t2_gini_disp` and `d_t1_life_exp_yrs`/`d_t2_life_exp_yrs`), and `treat_vars` (lines 100 - 108) omits baseline `income_profit_tax_share_rev_pct`.
   - Third, while `05_run_longitudinal_bcf_tax.R` rightly removes the target outcome's own baseline level $Y_{i,0}$ from $\mu_0(X_{i,t})$ to avoid tautological splitting at Wave 0, it also removes $Y_{i,0}$ (and the other three baseline tax variables) from the growth forests $\mu_1(\cdot)$ and $\mu_2(\cdot)$, depriving the longitudinal growth trees of baseline tax-level mean-reversion controls that are crucial in `mvbcf`.

   *Actionable fix:* Modify `05_run_longitudinal_bcf_tax.R` so that (a) the Wave 1 growth forest $\mu_1$ conditions strictly on $[t, t+1]$ mediators (`d_t1_*`) while the Wave 2 growth forest $\mu_2$ conditions on $[t+1, t+2]$ mediators, (b) all four mediators (including Gini and life expectancy changes) are included to match Model `M2`, and (c) baseline tax structure variables (or at least the non-target baseline tax shares) are included in the growth forests $\mu_1$ and $\mu_2$. Then re-run `lbcf` and explicitly discuss in Section 5.3 any remaining differences between `lbcf` and `mvbcf` on `Direct Income Tax / GDP`.

5. **Post-Treatment Mediator Conditioning (`M2`) vs Sequential G-Estimation and Intermediate Collider Bias.**
   To estimate the Mediator-Removed Direct Fiscal Effect (`M2`), `04_run_mvbcf_tax_models.R` (lines 320 - 321) simply appends the four post-treatment mediator changes $M_{i,t+h}$ (`d_t2_log_gdp_pc_pct`, `d_t2_tert_enrol_pp`, `d_t2_gini_disp`, `d_t2_life_exp_yrs`) as extra columns of the prognostic forest matrix `X_con` alongside $X_{i,t}$ and $\hat{\pi}(X_{i,t})$. While the manuscript cites Acharya, Blackwell & Sen (2016), Imai, Keele & Tingley (2010), and VanderWeele (2016), Acharya et al. (2016) specifically warn against naive single-stage regression conditioning on post-treatment mediators when unobserved post-treatment shocks affect both the mediator and the outcome. In a ten-year macroeconomic window, unobserved post-treatment shocks $U_{i,t+h}$ (such as tax administration modernisation, commodity price cycles, or statutory tax-rate reforms occurring between $t$ and $t+2$) simultaneously affect GDP growth ($\Delta \log \text{GDP/pc}_{t+h}$) and fiscal receipts ($\Delta Y_{i,t+h}$). Conditioning directly on $\Delta \log \text{GDP/pc}_{t+h}$ inside $\mu(X_{i,t}, M_{i,t+h}, \hat{\pi}_{i,t})$ opens a collider path $Z_{i,t} \to M_{i,t+h} \leftarrow U_{i,t+h} \to \Delta Y_{i,t+h}$ and allows regression trees in $\mu(\cdot)$ to form multi-way interactions between pre-treatment covariates $X_{i,t}$ and post-treatment mediators $M_{i,t+h}$.

   *Actionable fix:* Implement a proper two-stage sequential g-estimation benchmark following Acharya et al. (2016): in Stage 1, regress $\Delta Y_{i,t+h}$ on $M_{i,t+h}$, $Z_{i,t}$, and $X_{i,t}$ to estimate the mediator effect $\hat{\gamma}(M_{i,t+h})$; construct the demediated outcome $\widetilde{\Delta Y}_{i,t+h} = \Delta Y_{i,t+h} - \hat{\gamma}(M_{i,t+h})$; and in Stage 2, fit `mvbcf` on $\widetilde{\Delta Y}_{i,t+h}$ using strictly pre-treatment covariates $X_{i,t}$ and $\hat{\pi}(X_{i,t})$. Report this sequential g-estimation specification alongside `M2` and discuss the sensitivity of the direct effect to intermediate confounding.

6. **Competing Explanations, Uncontrolled Pre-Treatment Drift (`R5`) on Direct Income Tax Share, Missing Falsification Tests, and Mismatch Between Claimed and Coded Baseline Controls.**
   The manuscript argues that the placebo pre-trend test (`R5` in Table 6) and the negative-control outcome (`Goods & Services Tax Share`) rule out reverse causality and secular fiscal modernisation. Closer inspection reveals three weaknesses in how competing explanations are tested:
   - First, in Table 6 (`R5`, `Placebo (\Delta Y_{t-1 \to t})`), while the pre-treatment placebo estimate on `Direct Income Tax / GDP` is small ($+0.07$ pp, $P = 0.68$), the pre-treatment placebo estimate on the headline outcome `Direct Income Tax Share` is **$+0.58$ percentage points with $P(\tau > 0) = 0.854$** (more than half of the $+5$-year total effect of $+1.08$ pp), and the pre-treatment placebo on `Non-Resource Tax / GDP` is $+0.20$ pp ($P = 0.734$, five times larger than the $+5$-year post-treatment effect of $+0.04$ pp). Because `base_con_vars` in `04_run_mvbcf_tax_models.R` (lines 94 - 114) controls only for the wave-$t$ *level* of tax revenue and tax shares rather than the lagged pre-treatment *change* $\Delta Y_{i,t-1 \to t}$, countries with $Z_{i,t} = 1$ appear to be riding an ongoing pre-treatment trend toward direct income taxation (driven, for example, by concurrent tax administration reforms, electronic filing rollouts, or secular formalisation).
   - Second, because `Direct Income Tax Share` and `Goods & Services Tax Share` are components of total revenue that sum to $100\%$ alongside trade taxes, social contributions, and non-tax revenue, a positive shift in `Direct Income Tax Share` mechanically crowds out other revenue shares (as reflected in their $-0.559$ residual correlation). Thus, a negative sign on `Goods & Services Tax Share` is a compositional accounting reflection rather than an independent falsification test of concurrent fiscal reforms.
   - Third, Section 4.1 (line 188) explicitly claims that the prognostic forest $X_{i,t}$ controls for **"inflation, trade openness, natural resource rents, external debt, institutional democracy, and SWIID Gini inequality"**. Yet inspecting `base_con_vars` in `src/r/04_run_mvbcf_tax_models.R` (lines 94 - 114) and `base_vars` in `src/r/02_build_clean_tax_panel.R` (lines 126 - 158) shows that **inflation, external debt, and institutional democracy (or the Worldwide Governance Indicators `GE.EST` and `RL.EST` listed in `PROVENANCE.md` and cited via Kaufmann et al., 2011) are completely absent from the code and models**.

   *Actionable fix:* First, correct Section 4.1 (line 188) to match the exact 19 covariates in `base_con_vars`, and add the downloaded Worldwide Governance Indicators (`GE.EST` for Government Effectiveness and `RL.EST` for Rule of Law, which are already in `data/raw/wb_unesco_imf_wgi_long.csv`) plus lagged fiscal pre-trends ($\Delta Y_{i,t-1 \to t}$) into `base_con_vars` to absorb concurrent administrative state-capacity trajectories and pre-treatment drift. Second, add a non-compositional external falsification check (for example, testing whether tertiary education spending predicts forward changes in natural resource rents `% of GDP` or indirect goods and services tax as a `% of GDP`, `d_t2_goods_tax_gdp_pp`, which is already computed in `02_build_clean_tax_panel.R` line 314).

### 3. Minor concerns

1. **Global Median Imputation of Missing Baseline Covariates Across Income Tiers (`prep_matrix` in `04_run_mvbcf_tax_models.R`).**
   While treatments and outcomes are strictly observed, `prep_matrix()` (lines 132 - 142 of `04_run_mvbcf_tax_models.R`) imputes missing baseline covariates (such as `gross_tert_enrolment_pct`, which is missing in $10.5\%$ of the $+5$-year estimation sample per `eda_table4_missingness_analysis.csv`, and `trade_openness_gdp_pct`, missing in $6.5\%$) using the **pooled global sample median**. Assigning a global median tertiary enrolment rate ($\sim 30\%$) to a low-income country with missing enrolment data places that observation into the wrong enrolment regime in $\mu(X)$. Imputation in `prep_matrix()` and `prep_matrix_oof()` should be performed using the country's prior wave or its **World Bank income-group median** rather than the pooled global median.

2. **Inconsistency in Figure 1 (`fig1_structural_schematic.pdf`) Count of Tertiary Spending Measures.**
   Box 1 of Figure 1 is titled *"1. Tertiary Education Spend (Three Unbundled Measures)"* and lists three measures (Per-Student Spending Ratio, Real PPP Dollars per Student, and Tertiary Budget % of GDP). However, Section 1 (line 82), Section 3.3 (line 172), Section 5.2 (Table 3), and Figure 3 evaluate **four** tertiary spending definitions (including *2. Observed Per-Student Expansion*). Box 1 of Figure 1 should be updated to list all four measures so the schematic matches the rest of the paper.

3. **Rounding Discrepancies Between Figure 2 (`fig2_total_vs_direct_tax_posteriors.pdf`) and Table 2 (`tab:main_ate`).**
   In Figure 2 Panel 4 (`Goods & Services Tax Share`), the text annotations above the green (`M2`) intervals display `-0.21 pp (P>0: 36%)` at $+5$ years and `-0.34 pp (P>0: 35%)` at $+10$ years, whereas Table 2 (line 238), the abstract (line 52), and the main text (line 256) report **$-0.22$ pp** and **$-0.35$ pp**. Similarly, in Panel 3 at $+5$ years (`M1`), Figure 2 prints `P>0: 98%` whereas Table 2 prints `P=0.99`. This occurs because `06_generate_figures_and_diagnostics.R` formats values from raw draws/3-decimal summaries where `-0.215` and `-0.345` round to even. Harmonise the rounding rules across Table 2 and Figure 2.

4. **Omitted Rows in Table 4 (`tab:subgroups`) Referenced in the Main Text (line 371) and Unexplained Negative OECD CATEs.**
   On line 371, the text discusses the $+10$-year CATE for **Lower-Middle Income** countries ($+0.99$ pp of GDP), and Figure 4 plots all four World Bank income groups. Yet Table 4 (`tab:subgroups`, lines 338 - 367) omits the `Low income` ($N = 15, C = 9$, $+1.05$ pp) and `Lower middle income` ($N = 47, C = 21$, $+0.99$ pp) rows that exist in `outputs/tables/table3_subgroup_cates.csv`, jumping straight from the three enrolment regimes to `4. Upper Middle Income` and `5. High Income`. In addition, Table 4 reports `6. OECD Nordic High-Public` ($-0.80$ pp on `Non-Resource Tax / GDP`, $P = 0.03$), while omitting the other four OECD financing regimes from `table3_subgroup_cates.csv` (all of which also have negative CATEs between $-0.58$ and $-0.69$ pp on `Non-Resource Tax / GDP` because `m4_oecd_10y` is fitted on a small OECD-only subsample of $N = 119$). Include all four World Bank income groups in Table 4 and explain why the separate OECD-only subsample fit (`m4_oecd_10y`) produces negative point estimates on total tax-to-GDP across every OECD tuition regime.

5. **Conflicting Geweke $z$-Score and Effective Sample Size (ESS) Formulas Across Scripts (`04_run_mvbcf_tax_models.R` vs `06_generate_figures_and_diagnostics.R`) and Low ESS ($\text{ESS} = 58$) in Table 8.**
   The codebase defines two incompatible functions for MCMC convergence diagnostics: `calc_geweke_z()` in `04_run_mvbcf_tax_models.R` (line 164) divides by $\sqrt{\text{var}(x_1)/n_1 + \text{var}(x_2)/n_2}$, yielding large Geweke $z$-scores in `table1_mvbcf_ate_posteriors.csv` (e.g., $z = -3.88$ for `M2b` Outcome 1, $z = +7.60$ for `M1b` Outcome 4, and $z = -8.30$ for `M3f` Outcome 4), whereas `compute_geweke_z()` in `06_generate_figures_and_diagnostics.R` (line 570) divides by autocorrelation-adjusted effective sample sizes $\text{ess}_1$ and $\text{ess}_2$, reducing the reported Geweke $z$-score in Appendix Table 8 (`tab:mcmc`) to $-1.25$ for `M2b` Outcome 1. Furthermore, in Table 8 Row 8 (`Goods Tax Share`, $+10\text{y}$ `M2`), the effective sample size is only **$\text{ESS} = 58$** out of 1,250 post-burn-in draws. Reconcile the two diagnostic functions (using standard `coda::geweke.diag` and `coda::effectiveSize`) and increase the MCMC chain length from 2,000 to at least 5,000 iterations (with 1,500 burn-in) so that all outcomes achieve $\text{ESS} > 250$.

6. **Severe Sample Attrition in Low-Income Countries and External Validity Scope Conditions.**
